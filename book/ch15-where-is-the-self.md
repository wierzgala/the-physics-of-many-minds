# Chapter 15. Where is the self?

## The taste of someone else's drink

Krista and Tatiana Hogan were born in Vancouver in 2006, joined at the head. Scans showed a strand of tissue running between the two brains, linking the thalamus of one girl to the thalamus of the other. Their neurosurgeon called it a thalamic bridge (1). The thalamus is the relay station through which most sensory signals pass on their way to the cortex.

Their family noticed the consequences early. One girl would drink and the other would react. As they grew, it became clear that each could feel touches on the other's body and taste what the other was eating, and tests with one twin's eyes covered showed that each could report what the other was looking at (2). One twin dislikes a food the other enjoys, and protests when her sister eats it (2).

And yet nobody who knows them doubts that there are two of them. They have different personalities and different preferences. They argue. Each says "I".

The twins confound an assumption so basic that it is rarely stated: that an experience belongs to exactly one person. This chapter asks what the theory has to say about where one self ends and another begins. It also has to face the question the book has been circling since its first chapter. The swarm decides, humanity thinks, a species learns. Is any of them *someone*?

## A boundary made of traffic

Chapter 4 introduced a pure number called outside against inside: the information crossing a collective's outer boundary, divided by the information flowing across a cut through its middle. Chapter 9 put it in a table of three conditions for being one: a collective is a self, the theory proposes, while the traffic inside it far outruns the traffic across its edge.

For a single person the number is extreme. The two halves of your brain are joined by more than 300 million fibres (3), carrying dense traffic in both directions. Between you and another person, although your eyes take in millions of bits per second from the world, mutual two-way exchange is bottlenecked by each person's output funnel: speech at about 39 bits per second (4), plus gesture and touch. Bidirectional traffic drops by six to eight orders of magnitude at the skin (our estimate). That cliff is the boundary. On this view the self is not a thing located somewhere inside the head. It is the region enclosed by the steepest drop in the flow of information.

Three features of this definition matter.

**It is a matter of degree.** A ratio can take any value. The theory therefore expects in-between cases, and it expects them to be strange in exactly the way the Hogan twins are strange. A thin bridge between two thalami is far less than the traffic within either brain, so there are two selves. It is far more than speech, so some experiences cross.

**It should be counted in meaning, not raw bits.** Chapter 6 showed that shared context multiplies what each bit conveys. That is why the old couple of Chapter 6 are closer to being one system than two strangers are, though still immensely far from it.

**It can move.** If the boundary is a fact about traffic, then changing the traffic changes the self. The next two sections describe what happens when the traffic is cut, and when it is faked.

## Cutting the cable

From the 1940s onward, surgeons treated some cases of severe epilepsy by cutting the corpus callosum, to stop seizures spreading from one hemisphere to the other. Roger Sperry and Michael Gazzaniga then studied these "split-brain" patients in a famous series of experiments. Shown an object in the left half of the visual field, which is seen by the right hemisphere, a patient would say that he had seen nothing, since speech is usually controlled by the left hemisphere. But his left hand, controlled by the right hemisphere, could pick the object out. The textbook conclusion was that cutting the cable makes two minds in one skull (5).

The theory seems to predict exactly that. Remove the fibres, and the traffic across the middle of the brain collapses. Two regions of dense traffic are left with a thin channel between them. That should be two selves.

The facts turn out to be more interesting. In 2017 Yair Pinto and his colleagues retested two patients whose callosum had been completely cut, as confirmed by scans. As expected, the patients could not compare a stimulus on the left with one on the right: each hemisphere was processing its half of the world independently. But they could report that a stimulus was present, and where it was, anywhere in the visual field, and they could do it with either hand or in speech. The authors summed it up in their title: divided perception but undivided consciousness (6). The result rests on two patients and has been contested, and it should be held loosely.

If it stands, the theory reads it this way. Cutting the callosum removes most of the traffic between the hemispheres, and not all of it. They are still joined through deeper structures in the brainstem and midbrain. They share one body, one pair of eyes that move together, and one stream of behaviour, each of which carries information from one side to the other. What was lost is the wide channel needed to combine detailed perceptions. What may survive on the narrow channels is the thing Chapter 8 called the stage: the single broadcast that makes a mind act as one. A stage carries only about ten bits per second. It may not need 300 million fibres.

That suggests a refinement, and it is a claim: **being one self does not require sharing everything. It requires sharing one stage.** Perception can be divided while the stream of choice remains single. The measure of unity is not the total traffic but whether the parts take turns on one broadcast.

## The hand that is not yours

Now the opposite experiment: extend the self instead of dividing it.

In 1998 Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen seated volunteers at a table with one hand hidden behind a screen and a rubber hand in view in front of them. An experimenter stroked the hidden hand and the rubber hand with two small paintbrushes, in time with each other. Within minutes most people felt the touch as coming from the rubber hand, as if the rubber hand were sensing it. Asked afterwards to point to their own hand, they pointed toward the rubber one. When the two brushes were slightly out of time, the illusion largely vanished (7).

The experiment shows how the brain decides what is "me". It does not consult a fixed map. It looks for correlation. What I see being touched at the moment I feel a touch is part of me. In the language of this book, the brain draws the boundary of the self around whatever shares the most information with it, moment by moment. Ten minutes of synchronised brushing are enough to fool it into including a piece of rubber.

This means that there are two things we call the self, and they need to be kept apart.

- The **boundary** is a physical fact: where the traffic falls off.
- The **self-model** is the system's own running estimate of that boundary: what it takes itself to be.

Normally the two coincide. The rubber hand shows that the self-model can be stretched beyond the boundary by feeding it the right correlations. Chapter 6 found the same thing in couples, who become slow to say whether a trait belongs to themselves or their partner (8). A craftsman's tool, a driver's car and a musician's instrument are all taken into the self-model in the same way.

This resolves a puzzle from Chapter 6. A person can feel part of a team, a congregation or a nation, sometimes more intensely than they feel anything else. By the traffic test, none of those collectives is a self. Both statements can be true. The feeling of belonging is a fact about the *member's* self-model, which has extended to include the group. It is not evidence that the group has a self of its own. The flag moves you. Nothing is moved on the flag's side.

## Who is someone?

It is time to say plainly what this theory claims about consciousness, and what it does not.

It does not explain why any physical process is accompanied by experience at all. Nothing in the physics of bits, channels and energy tells us that. Any theory that claims otherwise should be distrusted, and this one makes no such claim.

What the theory offers is narrower: a list of physical conditions that a system must meet to be a single subject, a *someone*, if it is conscious at all. They have all appeared in earlier chapters.

1. **It must be integrated.** Its agents must talk about as much as they think, so that what one part does is available to the rest (Chapter 8).
2. **It must have a stage.** There must be a broadcast through which one content at a time is made available to the whole. The serial bottleneck in human choice is its signature (9, 10).
3. **It must have a boundary.** The traffic within must far exceed the traffic across its edge.
4. **It must be poised.** Its activity must be neither locked in step nor fragmented. The measure of Chapter 3, in which a magnetic pulse is given to the brain and the complexity of the echo is scored, separates conscious from unconscious states on just this basis (11).

These conditions overlap with two leading scientific theories of consciousness, the global workspace theory, which emphasises the broadcast (10), and integrated information theory, which emphasises integration and differentiation (12). The theory of this book does not choose between them. It adds the claim that the conditions are stated in terms that can be measured in any collective of agents, not only in brains.

Apply the list.

- **A waking human brain** meets all four, or more exactly its cortex and thalamus do.
- **The human cerebellum** offers a revealing internal contrast: it holds roughly four-fifths of the brain's 86 billion neurons (13), yet its circuitry is modular and largely feedforward, lacking a global broadcast stage. The rare people born without a cerebellum have difficulties with movement and coordination and are nevertheless conscious subjects. That is what the four conditions would lead one to expect: it is not the number of neurons that makes a self.
- **A brain in dreamless sleep or under anaesthesia** fails the fourth, and by the evidence of Chapter 3 no one is there.
- **A bee swarm, an ant colony, a market, a nation, humanity, a species** all fail the first. Each of their agents thinks vastly more than it says. They are federated minds. They process information, decide and learn. By this theory they are not subjects. There is thinking, and nobody is doing it.
- **A couple, a team, a crowd at a concert** fail the first and third. Their members' self-models overlap, which is what they feel. No new subject comes into being.

This is the answer to the oldest objection to the idea that societies think: that if a nation were a mind there would be something it is like to be France. The theory agrees that there is not, and says why. Intelligence requires agents, channels and a shared code. Being someone requires far more, and almost nothing above the level of a single brain has it.

## How two could become one

The conditions also say what it would take to make a new subject out of two. The channel between them would have to widen until the traffic across it was comparable to the traffic within each, and the two would have to come to share a single stage.

That gives the theory one of its sharpest predictions. Chapter 8 described the signature of a stage: when two choices have to be made at once, the second waits for the first. Two separate people given independent cues and separate hands show no such interference with each other; you can choose while I choose. If two brains were ever joined closely enough to become one subject, they should begin to show this central bottleneck *between* them even when their sensory inputs and motor effectors are completely separate: one brain's choice should delay the other's, as one hand's delays the other's in a single person. If they remain two, no such central delay should appear, however much sensation they share.

The test could in principle be run wherever brains are joined, by nature or, one day, by technology. It turns the question "one mind or two?" from a matter of philosophy into a matter of timing. On the evidence that is public, sharing sensations across a thalamic bridge while keeping separate wills and preferences is what the theory would expect of two subjects with an unusually wide channel between them, and not of one.

The same reasoning applies to the largest scale. Chapter 16 will argue that the Earth is small enough for a mind that thinks at human speed. Whether such a mind would be a subject depends on these four conditions and on nothing else. Widening humanity's channels would not be enough. It would also need a stage, and a stage has room for one thing at a time. A planet that was truly someone would have to think one thought at a time. Whether that would be a gain is a question the theory can pose and cannot answer.

## In short

- A self is the region enclosed by the steepest drop in the flow of information. For a person, traffic falls by about a million-fold at the skin.
- The boundary is a matter of degree. Conjoined twins whose brains are linked by a bridge of tissue share sensations and remain two people.
- After the main cable between the hemispheres is cut, perception divides, but two carefully studied patients still behaved as single subjects. The theory's claim: unity requires sharing one stage, not sharing everything.
- The rubber hand illusion shows that the brain decides what is "me" by correlation, and can be fooled in minutes. The physical boundary and the self-model are different things. Belonging is the self-model extending to a group; it does not make the group a self.
- The theory does not explain why there is experience. It proposes four measurable conditions for being a single subject: integration, a stage, a boundary and a poised balance. Brains meet them. Swarms, nations, humanity and species do not.
- Prediction: two brains that became one subject would show a serial bottleneck between them.

## How we could be wrong

The definition of the self as a cliff in information flow would fail if clear cases of single subjects turned out to have no such cliff, or if systems with a sharp cliff and dense internal traffic showed no sign of unified behaviour. The claim that unity requires only a shared stage rests heavily on a small number of split-brain patients, and critics have argued that cross-cueing and low-bandwidth subcortical channels can explain the unified responses without undivided consciousness (14); if larger studies confirm the older picture of two independent minds, with separate and simultaneous streams of choice, the refinement is wrong and unity needs the wide channel after all. The four conditions would be undermined by evidence of unified experience in a system that plainly lacks one of them, though it is hard to say what such evidence would look like, and that difficulty is itself a weakness: claims about who is conscious are much harder to test than claims about who is intelligent. The bottleneck prediction is the most exposed. If closely joined brains show shared choice without interference, or interference without any other sign of unity, the proposed signature of a single subject is not one.
