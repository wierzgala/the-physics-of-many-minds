# Chapter 8. Two kinds of mind

## Eight arms, one animal

An octopus has about 500 million neurons, roughly as many as a dog. But a dog keeps most of its neurons in its head. An octopus keeps more than two-thirds of them in its arms and body (1). Each arm has its own long nerve cord, swelling into a chain of small ganglia, one for each sucker, and much of the work of sensing and moving is done there, close to the action (2).

The arms can act on their own to a remarkable degree. In 2001 a team in Jerusalem cut the connection between an arm and the brain and then stimulated the arm. It reached out with a movement almost identical to a normal one: the motor program for reaching lives in the arm itself (3). Severed arms go on grabbing objects for more than an hour. They will grab almost anything, with one striking exception: they do not grab the skin of an octopus, a chemical check that seems to stop an animal's own arms from getting tangled up in each other (4). The philosopher Peter Godfrey-Smith has described an octopus in an unfamiliar tank with food in the middle: some of its arms crowd into a corner, seeking safety, while others pull the animal toward the food (5).

Is an octopus one mind or nine? Its brain sets goals, and the arms work out how to meet them, mostly without reporting back. It is something between a brain and a committee.

That in-between case is the best way into this chapter. The book has so far described minds as if they came in one kind. They come in two, and the octopus has a foot, or rather an arm, in each.

## One number

Chapter 4 introduced a pure number and promised that it would separate two kinds of mind. It is the ratio of **thought to talk**: how much information an agent processes inside itself, divided by how much it exchanges with the other agents.

| Collective | Its agents | Thought against talk, roughly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cortex | neurons | close to one: a neuron computes at the very synapses where it communicates |
| Octopus | brain and eight arm cords | in between: arms do much locally and report little |
| Bee swarm | scouts | very large: a brain of a million neurons, a dance of about seven bits (6) |
| Human society | people | about a hundred million: a billion bits per second in, ten out (7) |

(The last column is our rough estimate.)

Call a collective **integrated** when the ratio is near one, and **federated** when it is far above one. In an integrated mind, communication and computation are the same activity; there is almost nothing an agent does that its neighbours do not, in some sense, hear. In a federated mind, each agent does a great deal that no one else ever learns of, and what crosses between agents is a summary: a dance, a pheromone trail, a vote, a price, a sentence.

This chapter argues that the two kinds differ in almost everything that matters: how fast they think, how many things they can think at once, what kind of ideas they produce, how they fail and how large they can grow.

## Why the number is what it is

The ratio is not arbitrary. It follows from what the agents are like and from the economics of Chapter 7.

A neuron is a simple agent. Its processing consists of adding up the signals that arrive at its synapses and deciding whether to fire. Almost everything it computes, it computes *in* the act of communicating. So a mind built from neurons is integrated almost by necessity.

A human being is a rich agent. Inside each of us is an integrated mind of 86 billion neurons, taking in about a billion bits per second from the senses. What leaves is behaviour, at about ten bits per second (7). This is the great funnel of Chapter 2, and it is what makes any mind built from people federated. Nearly all of what each person thinks stays inside.

Chapter 7 added two reasons why the funnel is not a defect to be engineered away. Energy favours many thin, slow channels over a few fat ones, because the cost of each bit rises with the rate. And as a collective grows, cross-talk grows more slowly than the number of agents, so a large mind cannot keep every part in close conversation anyway.

Put together, these give a rule, and it is a claim of the theory: **in biology, integrated minds are built from simple agents, and federated minds are built from rich ones.** Because every living animal's brain is sealed inside a body behind a narrow sensory and motor funnel, the two kinds have had to alternate up the hierarchy: simple neurons make an integrated brain, and rich brains make federated colonies, swarms and societies. Chapters 14 and 17 will ask what happens when artificial agents learn to pass internal states directly without squeezing them through words, breaking that alternation for the first time.

## The integrated mind: one thing at a time

Here is a puzzle about yourself. Your brain has billions of neurons working in parallel. Yet you cannot do two simple choices at once. When people are asked to respond to two stimuli in quick succession, the second response is reliably delayed, as if it had to wait its turn. Psychologists call this the psychological refractory period, and decades of experiments point to a stubborn bottleneck that covers choosing actions and, probably, retrieving memories (8). Jieyu Zheng and Markus Meister, who measured the ten-bit funnel, list this among the great unexplained facts of the brain: why can we only think about one thing at a time? (7)

The leading account of conscious access gives part of the answer. On the global workspace theory, information becomes conscious when a large network spanning the front and sides of the cortex suddenly "ignites", amplifying one representation and sustaining it so that it can be read by processors all over the brain (9, 10). The workspace is a broadcast. And a broadcast, by its nature, carries one programme at a time.

The theory of this book offers a reason why such a stage should exist at all, and why it should be single. An integrated mind is defined by its parts all taking part in one process, and an animal has only one body to commit to one action at a time. The way a mind of many agents acts as one is to put a single committed state in front of all of them at once. That *is* a broadcast, and a broadcast has one channel. Sensory areas can run massively in parallel behind the curtain, but at the level of choice, **the single stream of thought is the price of being one mind.** The ten bits per second that Zheng and Meister measured would then be the bandwidth of that stage, not of the brain behind it. This is the theory's claim, offered as an answer to their question.

The integrated mind buys a great deal with that price. It is fast: its agents can be coordinated within one crossing time. It can hold a single model of a situation and change the whole of it at once, which is what an insight feels like. And, if Chapter 15 is right, it can be someone: a self, with one point of view.

## The federated mind: many things at once

A federated mind pays none of that price, and gets none of the benefit.

In 2012 Takao Sasaki and Stephen Pratt gave colonies of *Temnothorax* ants a choice of new nests, either two nests or eight, and gave the same choice to single ants. Individual ants made much worse decisions when faced with eight options than with two: they suffered from cognitive overload, as people do. Whole colonies did not. In a colony, each ant inspects only a small fraction of the sites, and the comparison happens across ants, not inside any one of them (11).

A year later the same group, with collaborators in Uppsala, ran the opposite test: two nests whose quality was varied from nearly identical to obviously different. When the difference was small, colonies chose the better nest more often than single ants did. When the difference was large, single ants did better (12). The colony's positive feedback, ants recruiting ants, sharpens its sense of fine differences. On easy choices the same feedback can lock the whole colony onto the wrong option before anyone notices (12).

Both findings fit the picture. A federated mind thinks many things at once, because each agent thinks its own thing. It has no single stage and no bottleneck. It does not get overloaded, because no part of it ever sees the whole problem. It is robust, because no agent is essential. And it is slow, because its parts must agree by exchanging summaries, and because its decisions emerge from feedback that cannot be hurried without risking a stampede.

What it cannot do is concentrate. It has no single thought for the whole of it to be thinking. No one is home.

## The same people, two kinds of mind

Ants and neurons are fixed in their ways. People are not. The same human beings can be organised into an integrated group or a federated one, which lets us test the theory on ourselves.

In 2019 Lingfei Wu, Dashun Wang and James Evans analysed more than 65 million papers, patents and software products from 1954 to 2014. They measured, for each, whether it disrupted its field, redirecting attention away from the work it built on, or developed it, extending existing lines. Smaller teams tended to disrupt with new ideas; larger teams tended to develop existing ones, and the difference was greatest for the most influential work (13).

Read through this chapter, that is the signature of the two kinds of mind. A small team can be nearly integrated: everyone holds the whole problem, and the team can rebuild its picture of the world in a single conversation. That is what disruption requires. A large team is federated by necessity: the problem is divided, each member refines a piece, and pieces are joined through narrow interfaces. That is what development requires. Neither is better. Science, the study's authors concluded, needs both sizes of team (13). The theory says why: they are two different kinds of mind, suited to two different kinds of thought.

The structure shows up in what such minds make. In 1968 the programmer Melvin Conway observed that organisations which design systems end up producing designs that copy their own communication structure (14). The idea became known as Conway's law, and in 2012 Alan MacCormack, Carliss Baldwin and John Rusnak tested it. They compared pairs of software products that did the same job, one built by a tightly coupled commercial firm and one by a loosely coupled open-source community. In every pair, the product of the loosely coupled organisation was more modular, by up to a factor of six (15).

The theory reads this as a general law, and it is a claim: **the thoughts of a mind carry the shape of its communication.** Integrated minds produce integrated thoughts: new wholes, in which every part depends on every other. Federated minds produce modular thoughts: assemblies of parts that can be improved one at a time. A civilisation, being federated, is superb at improving things piece by piece and poor at changing its whole picture at once. When it does change its picture, the change usually starts in a small, nearly integrated group, and then spreads.

## Why one kind sleeps and the other does not

There is one more difference between the two kinds, so familiar in our own lives that its strangeness is easy to miss. Every night your brain disconnects from its senses, paralyses your body and goes offline for hours. Why should a mind ever shut down when predators are still awake?

Compare that with a fire-ant colony. Individual worker ants do sleep, taking more than two hundred brief naps a day of about a minute each, but they do so out of step with one another, so that most of the colony is awake at every moment and the nest never closes (16). Human cities and global markets do the same across time zones and shifts. Federated minds do not sleep as a whole. Integrated biological minds almost universally do.

Even more revealing are the animals whose worlds forbid shutting down. A bottlenose dolphin must keep swimming to surface and breathe, and a great frigatebird can fly non-stop across the ocean for ten days without landing on water. Neither gives up sleep. Instead, both sleep **one cerebral hemisphere at a time**: slow-wave sleep appears in half the cortex while the other half stays awake, keeping one eye open to steer and watch for danger (17, 18).

The earlier chapters supply a physical reason for all three facts, and it is a claim of the theory. Chapter 4 showed that a finite memory receiving a flood of input cannot record everything; it must continually compress its experience into statistics. Chapter 5 showed that compressing experience into better priors is how a mind raises its efficiency. In nerve tissue, a day of learning strengthens synapses across the cortex, raising energy costs, crowding memory and threatening to saturate the network unless connections are replayed, pruned and renormalised while sensory input is shut out (19).

Because an integrated mind has **one stage**, it faces a scheduling conflict. It cannot use that broadcast to couple tightly to a fast, dangerous world and at the same time run global offline replay across the network without mixing hallucination and perception. So it must take turns in time: awake to couple and spend capacity, asleep to compress the library and restore efficiency. A federated mind has no single stage, so its agents can maintain their libraries in staggered shifts while the collective stays open for business. And when an animal's niche makes going wholly offline fatal, evolution loosens the coupling across the midline during rest, turning one integrated brain temporarily into a two-node federation.

## Two kinds, side by side

Gathering the argument, the theory predicts these contrasts. Each row is a hypothesis that can be checked across brains, colonies, teams and societies.

| | Integrated | Federated |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thought against talk | near one | far above one |
| Agents | simple | rich, each a mind in itself |
| Speed | fast: one crossing time per step | slow: many rounds of summaries |
| Size | limited, by crossing time and wiring | almost unlimited |
| Thoughts at once | one, broadcast to all | many, one per agent or module |
| Typical product | new wholes; insight; disruption | improved parts; development; modules |
| Signals between agents | dense, continuous | sparse summaries: dances, prices, votes, papers |
| Overload | yes, at the single stage | rarely; no part sees the whole |
| Robustness to losing agents | lower; damage spreads | high; agents are replaceable |
| Rest and maintenance | global offline sleep | staggered shifts; the whole never sleeps |
| Characteristic failure | seizure: everyone in lockstep | stampede or fragmentation |
| A self? | possible | absent |
| Best suited to | a fast, local world | a slow, wide world |

The last row connects this chapter to the coherence number of Chapter 4. A mind must be integrated to act as one on a fast world, and it can only be integrated if it is small. A mind that must cover a wide world must be federated, and then it is slow. There is no design that is large, fast and unified at once. Chapter 16 finds the same choice at the scale of planets: one can be unified and present, or vast and plural, but not both.

## The octopus answered

So is the octopus one mind or nine? The theory's answer is that the question has a measurable answer, and the answer is "partly". Where the traffic between an arm and the brain is rich compared with what the arm does alone, the octopus is one integrated mind. Where an arm does a great deal and reports little, the octopus is a small federation. Real minds sit along the scale, not at its ends.

Your own brain is not at the end either. Its two hemispheres are joined by hundreds of millions of fibres, but Chapter 7 showed that signals between them are slow, and some functions are divided between the sides. Chapter 15 will ask what happens when the fibres between the hemispheres are cut, and whether a person can become two.

And the scale can be travelled. A federated mind becomes more integrated if its channels widen relative to what its agents do inside, and an integrated mind becomes federated if its channels thin. Humanity's channels are widening for the first time in its history, through translation between languages and machines that talk at millions of bits per second. Chapter 17 asks where on the scale that could carry us.

## In short

- The ratio of thought to talk, what an agent processes inside divided by what it exchanges, separates two kinds of mind. Near one, a mind is integrated, like a cortex. Far above one, it is federated, like a swarm, a colony or a society.
- Integrated minds are built from simple agents, federated minds from rich ones, so the two kinds alternate up the hierarchy. The human funnel, a billion bits per second in and ten out, makes any society of people federated.
- People can only do one thing at a time at the level of choice, a stubborn bottleneck. The theory's claim: a mind acts as one by broadcasting, and a broadcast carries one programme, so a single stream of thought is the price of integration.
- Ant colonies avoid the overload that single ants suffer, because each ant sees only part of the problem. Colonies beat individuals on fine discriminations and lose on easy ones.
- In 65 million papers, patents and software products, small teams disrupted and large teams developed. Loosely coupled organisations build more modular software, by up to a factor of six. The theory's claim: a mind's thoughts carry the shape of its communication.
- Integrated biological minds go offline to sleep; federated collectives rest in shifts, and dolphins and frigatebirds sleep one hemisphere at a time. The theory's claim: a mind with one stage must alternate online coupling with offline library compression, unless it temporarily federates its halves.
- No mind can be large, fast and unified at once. Real minds, from the octopus to the human brain, sit along the scale between the two kinds and can move along it as their channels change.

## How we could be wrong

The chapter's central claim is that one ratio predicts a cluster of properties. It would fail if collectives with similar ratios of thought to talk turned out to differ widely in speed, capacity for many simultaneous tasks, robustness and type of output, or if collectives with very different ratios behaved alike. The claim that a single stream of thought is the price of integration predicts that any integrated mind, natural or artificial, will show a serial bottleneck at the level where it acts as a whole, and that federated minds will not. An integrated system that made several unified choices at once, or a federated one with a true single stage, would count against it. The sleep argument would fail if continually learning, finite-memory integrated minds, biological or artificial, proved able to compress and maintain their priors indefinitely at full waking capacity without offline periods, or if unihemispheric sleepers showed undiminished cross-hemisphere integration while half asleep. The reading of team size as a move along the scale predicts that what matters is the density of communication relative to individual work, not headcount as such. A small team whose members rarely talk should behave like a federated mind, and a large team in constant, dense contact should behave like an integrated one. If disruption tracks headcount alone, regardless of how the team communicates, the theory's explanation is wrong.
