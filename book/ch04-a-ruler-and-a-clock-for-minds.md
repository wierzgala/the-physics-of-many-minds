# Chapter 4. A ruler and a clock for minds

## The secret in the photographs

In 1947 the United States Army released a series of photographs of the first atomic explosion, the Trinity test of July 1945. They showed a ball of fire swelling over the New Mexico desert. Each frame carried a time stamp and a scale bar. The photographs went round the world's newspapers. The energy of the bomb remained a secret (1).

In England the fluid dynamicist Geoffrey Ingram Taylor looked at the pictures and saw that the secret was printed on them. A blast wave spreading into air can depend on very few things: the energy released, the density of the air, and the time since the explosion. There is only one way to combine those three quantities into a length. So the radius of the fireball at a known time fixes the energy, which grows as the fifth power of the radius (1). Taylor published his estimate in 1950, and it was close to the true yield of about 20 kilotons (1, 2).

The story is usually told as a triumph of units alone, and that is too simple. Taylor had worked out the physics of blast waves in detail years earlier, and the units cannot supply the numerical factor that his full calculation did (2, 3). But the heart of the popular story is true. Asking only *what could this possibly depend on, and in what units?* takes you most of the way to the answer, before you know any of the details.

This is called dimensional analysis. Its central theorem, proved by Edgar Buckingham in 1914, says that any physical law can be rewritten as a relation between pure numbers, combinations of the variables in which all the units cancel (3). Those pure numbers are what the system is really governed by. The rest is a choice of ruler.

This chapter does to minds what Taylor did to the fireball. We list what a collective mind could possibly depend on, with units, and see what pure numbers fall out. We do not yet know the detailed laws of intelligence. As with the bomb, we may not need them to learn a great deal.

## What a mind is made of, with units

Chapter 2 established that information is a physical quantity. So a mind built from agents can be described with three kinds of unit: bits, seconds and metres. Energy joins them in Chapter 7.

| Quantity | Units | Human brain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number of agents | a pure count | about 86 billion neurons (4) |
| Links per agent | a pure count | thousands of synapses |
| Memory per agent | bits | tens of thousands of bits, at about 4.7 bits per synapse (5) |
| Capacity of one link | bits per second | a few bits per second |
| Spacing between agents | metres | a few hundredths of a millimetre |
| Signal speed | metres per second | from under 1 to about 100 |
| Pace of the world it deals with | seconds | tenths of a second, for catching and dodging |

The entries without a reference are rough working values of ours. Nothing below depends on their precision. What matters is which combinations of them have no units at all.

## The first clock

Start with time. For most of history, thought was assumed to be instantaneous. As late as the 1840s the great physiologist Johannes Müller believed that the speed of the nerve signal was comparable to the speed of light, and that it would never be measured (6).

His student Hermann von Helmholtz measured it within a few years, with a frog's leg, a battery and a clever timing device. He stimulated a nerve at two different distances from the muscle and timed the difference in the twitch. The answer was astonishingly slow: roughly 25 to 40 metres per second (7, 8). In later experiments on people he found about 60 metres per second (9). A signal from your toe takes longer to reach your brain than a sound takes to cross a room.

This was the discovery that minds have a clock. If signals have a finite speed, a mind of a given size has a **crossing time**: its size divided by its signal speed. Nothing in the mind can be coordinated faster than that. For a human brain, some 15 centimetres across, with signals that mostly travel at a few metres per second and pause at every synapse, the crossing time is a few hundredths of a second.

Size, therefore, costs time. A study of land mammals from shrews to elephants measured every component of the fastest reflex and found that the total delay grows steadily with body mass. In the largest animals it is about 17 times longer than in the smallest (10). One of the authors pointed out that a signal takes longer to travel from an elephant's spinal cord to its lower leg than from an orbiting satellite to the ground (11).

Yet elephants do not stumble through life. The same study found that big animals also *move* more slowly, so that the delay as a fraction of the movement is only about twice as long in an elephant as in a shrew (11). Evolution has kept a ratio nearly constant while the absolute times changed seventeen-fold.

That is a pure number, held steady across four orders of magnitude of body mass. It is the first hint of what this book is looking for.

## The coherence number

Call it the **coherence number**: the pace of the world a mind is dealing with, divided by the mind's crossing time. It answers one question. Can the whole mind confer before the situation changes?

A fastball in professional baseball reaches the plate in about 400 milliseconds (12). The batter's brain needs about 80 milliseconds just to get the image to the frontal lobes, and the swing has to begin by about 250 milliseconds (12). Divide the ball's flight by the brain's crossing time and you get a coherence number of roughly ten. The whole brain can exchange signals about ten times while the ball is in the air. That is enough, barely, and it is why hitting a fastball is among the hardest things a human body does.

When the number falls below one, something specific happens: the mind cannot act as a unit on that problem. Its parts must act alone. You see this in your own body. When you touch a hot stove, your hand pulls back before you feel the pain, because the decision was made in the spinal cord. The full brain was too far away, in time, to be consulted.

The swarm of Chapter 1 has a crossing time of tens of minutes, the round trip of a scout. It could never catch a ball. But its problem, a tree cavity that will stay where it is for years, changes far more slowly than the swarm ticks. Its coherence number is enormous. The swarm is a slow mind well matched to a slow problem.

So the first pure number already yields a law in outline. **A collective can think as one only about things that change more slowly than signals cross it.** Chapter 13 applies this to planets and galaxies, where the signal is light and the law becomes a wall.

It also explains a curious fact about experience. Perception does not come in instants. Experiments show that a sight and a sound are bound into one event if they arrive within a window of up to several hundred milliseconds of each other (13). The theory's reading is that this window is the brain's crossing time made visible: the shortest interval within which the brain can have agreed with itself about what happened. That reading is a hypothesis. The window is a measured fact.

## The first ruler

Now space. Imagine slicing a mind in two with a flat cut and asking how much information can flow across the cut per second. Engineers who design supercomputers ask exactly this question and call the answer the bisection bandwidth. It is a quantity measured through a surface: bits per second per square metre of cut.

It matters because of a simple piece of geometry. If agents talk only to their near neighbours, the links crossing a cut are those of the agents lying along it. The number of agents grows with the *volume* of the mind. The number along a cut grows only with its *area*. Double the width of such a mind and you have eight times the agents but only four times the cross-talk. Each half knows less and less, relatively, about the other. A big mind built only from local links comes apart.

Brains show the escape, and its price. They add long-range cables: white matter, bundles of axons running beneath the grey matter where the computing is done. The largest of them, the corpus callosum, joins the two hemispheres with more than 300 million fibres (14). And the cables cost more as the brain grows. Across mammals from the pygmy shrew to the elephant, the volume of white matter rises as the 4/3 power of the volume of grey matter (15). A brain ten times bigger needs about twenty times the cabling. Large brains are increasingly made of wire.

That 4/3 law was predicted from a simple assumption about wiring economy and then found to fit data across several orders of magnitude of brain size (15). The trade-off between talking across the whole and paying for the cables turns out to have a common solution. Human brain networks, the 302-neuron nervous system of the worm *C. elegans*, and high-performance computer chips all follow the same scaling rule relating a region's size to its outgoing connections, known to chip designers as Rent's rule (16). Evolution and engineers, facing the same geometry, arrived at the same compromise.

There is a second escape: make the agents smaller. Parrots and songbirds pack about twice as many neurons into a brain of a given mass as primates do, and crows and parrots reach ape-like feats of cognition with brains the size of a walnut (17). Shorter distances mean shorter crossing times and less cable. Density is not the same thing as size, and a theory of minds needs both.

## Three more ratios

The list of quantities gives a few more pure numbers. Each is introduced here and put to work later.

**Talk against thought.** Divide what an agent can process by what it can communicate. In a brain the two are nearly equal, because a neuron's computing is done at its synapses, the very places where it communicates. In a swarm or a society the ratio is immense: each member thinks vastly more than it can say. Chapter 8 shows that this single number separates two kinds of mind.

**Memory against input.** Divide what an agent can store by what flows into it. A brain with some 10¹⁵ bits of storage (5) receives about ten million bits per second from the eyes alone (18). At that rate it would fill in a few years. So a brain cannot record. It must compress, keeping the statistics of its experience and throwing away nearly all of the experience itself. Forgetting is forced by arithmetic.

**Outside against inside.** Divide the traffic across a collective's outer boundary by the traffic across a cut through its middle. Between the two halves of your brain run those 300 million fibres. Between you and another person runs speech, at about 39 bits per second (19). The ratio differs by many orders of magnitude, and Chapter 12 will argue that this number is where a self begins and ends. Chapter 6 adds a correction: bits are not meaning.

## The method, and its promise

Collect the results. From a list of what a mind is made of, with units, we have extracted a handful of pure numbers: the coherence number, the scaling of cross-talk with size, talk against thought, memory against input, outside against inside. None required a theory of what thinking is.

What Buckingham's theorem promises is that any law of collective intelligence, whatever it turns out to be, can be written in terms of numbers like these. That has a consequence worth stating boldly. If two minds have the same pure numbers, then nothing in physics distinguishes what they are doing, whatever they are made of and however large or slow they are. An ant colony, a brain and a civilisation could, in principle, be placed on the same chart. Chapter 10 follows that idea to its conclusion.

Taylor needed only a ruler and a clock to find the energy of the bomb. For minds, we now have both.

## In short

- Dimensional analysis asks what a system could depend on, with units. The combinations in which the units cancel are the numbers that govern it.
- Signals in minds have finite speed, so every mind has a crossing time, and size costs time. Reflex delays in mammals grow about 17-fold from shrew to elephant.
- The coherence number is the pace of the world divided by the crossing time. Below one, a collective cannot act as a unit. For a batter facing a fastball it is about ten.
- Information flow through a cut grows with area while agents grow with volume. Big minds must pay for long-range wiring, and in mammals that cost rises as the 4/3 power of the computing tissue.
- Brains, a worm's nervous system and computer chips follow the same wiring rule. Birds show that density is a separate lever from size.
- Any law of collective intelligence can be written in terms of pure numbers. Minds with the same numbers are, physically, doing the same thing.

## How we could be wrong

Dimensional analysis only finds the numbers that follow from the variables you list. If a variable that matters is missing, such as the shared context of Chapter 6, the numbers will mislead. The claim that the coherence number must exceed one for unified action would be refuted by a collective that reliably acts as a whole faster than signals can cross it. The reading of the perceptual binding window as the brain's crossing time predicts that the window should be wider in larger or slower brains and narrower in smaller or faster ones. If it is the same in all species, the reading is wrong.
