# Chapter 19. How we could be wrong

## A theory's duty

A theory earns its keep by forbidding things. The philosopher Karl Popper made this the test of science: a claim that is compatible with every possible observation tells us nothing about the world (1). Ideas about collective minds have a poor record on this score. It is easy to say that an anthill is like a brain, or that humanity is waking up, and impossible to say what would count against it.

This book has tried to do the opposite. Every chapter ended by naming the observation that would undermine it. This last chapter gathers those into one place, sorts them by how exposed they are, and says honestly where the theory is weakest. It also follows the advice of the biophysicist John Platt, who argued that fields advance fastest when they design experiments to decide between rival hypotheses and not to confirm a favourite (2).

The preface said that this theory was assembled by a small, tightly coupled mind and now needs a large, loosely coupled one. This chapter is the handover. It is written as a list of things for other people to break.

## What is established, what is claimed, what is guessed

Three kinds of statement have been kept apart throughout. It is worth seeing how much of the book rests on each.

**Established findings**, on which the theory builds and which do not depend on it:

- Information has an energy cost, confirmed in the laboratory, and each bit gathered buys at most one bit of control.
- Honeybee swarms and brains both decide by competing populations that accumulate evidence, inhibit each other and stop at a threshold.
- Crowds are accurate when their members' errors are independent, and lose accuracy under social influence.
- Brains show scale-free avalanches, and the complexity of their response to a pulse separates conscious from unconscious states.
- People take in about a billion bits per second and act at about ten.
- Signals in nerves are slow, and reflex delays grow with body size.
- Shared context reduces the bits needed to communicate, and groups with more of it perform better than their members' abilities predict.
- The energy cost of a bit in the fly's eye rises with the rate at which it is sent.
- Small teams tend to disrupt and large teams to develop; loosely coupled organisations build more modular products.
- Protein structures can be predicted from known sequences by computation alone; compatible language models can pass internal activations directly at lower cost than text.
- Fire-ant colonies sleep in staggered shifts while dolphins and frigatebirds sleep one hemisphere at a time; aircraft factories lose tacit skill when turnover outruns onboarding; rove beetles mimic ant colony codes, and costly handicaps stabilise honesty between rivals.

**The theory's claims**, which could be false while all of the above stayed true:

- Intelligent output is capacity times an efficiency (gathering times extraction) that cannot exceed one.
- Unity is a ratio of rates, in three forms: acting as one, being a self, staying one kind (including institutional amnesia when staff churn outruns onboarding).
- Minds are built from many slow agents because that is the cheapest way to move information.
- The ratio of thought to talk sorts minds into integrated and federated kinds, with different speeds, products, failures and patterns of rest: integrated minds must go offline to sleep, while federated minds consolidate in shifts.
- The same thought can occur at different speeds in minds with matching pure numbers.
- Levels of mind are marked by breaks in scaling laws, and an agent is a scale at which a summary is enough.
- A collective should maximise both code alignment (κ) and error independence (N_eff / N); when interests diverge, honest signals must be costly, and high κ without border checks lowers the cost of code hijacking.
- Every species is a mind; nations are not; humanity is one, and is no one.
- Artificial neural networks are integrated minds with a finite stage, and artificial intelligence will develop as federations of them (with compatible units sometimes fused directly in latent space), not as one giant mind.
- A task fixes the outline of the mind that can perform it: input rate sets width, compression sets depth, deadline sets size, sparsity and repertoire set bulk, and correlated cues with deadlines fix a finite best group size N*.

**Speculation**, flagged as such where it appeared:

- That belonging is what participation in a larger mind feels like.
- The portrait of a galactic mind.
- An integrated core of machines with humanity as its periphery.
- Relative actuality, and everything in Chapter 18.

A reader who rejects all the speculation loses the last third of the fun and none of the theory. A reader who rejects the claims is left with the established findings, which were worth assembling in any case.

## Where the theory is weakest

It would be dishonest to present only the tests the theory proposes for itself. Here are the objections that the author finds hardest to answer.

**The word "mind" may be doing too much.** The book calls a swarm, a species and a market minds because they store information, process it and reach decisions. A critic can fairly say that this stretches the word until it covers any adaptive system, and that the resemblances are analogies. The reply is that the theory makes numerical predictions across these systems, such as the collapse of decision curves. If those fail, the critic is right, and "mind" was a metaphor.

**The definition of efficiency may be the wrong one.** The ceiling of one, and everything said about superintelligence, depends on measuring achievement in bits of uncertainty removed. Real tasks are not all of that kind. If no general measure of achievement can be defined, the central formula is a slogan.

**Many of the pure numbers were estimated, not measured.** The ratio of thought to talk, the context amplification of a couple, the boundary ratio of a self: the book gives rough values and argues from them. Until someone measures them properly, the arguments have the status of plausible arithmetic.

**Some of the evidence is thin.** The claim that thin channels are cheaper per bit rests mainly on insect photoreceptors. The refinement about split brains rests on two patients. The account of conjoined twins rests on journalism. Several of the scaling laws are disputed on statistical grounds. Each of these was flagged where it appeared, and each could give way.

**The theory explains failure too easily.** Too much coupling is bad, and too little is bad. A sceptic will notice that this lets the theory account for any collective failure after the fact, by declaring it to have been on one side or the other. Separating code alignment (κ) from error independence (N_eff / N) in Chapters 6 and 12 turns that qualitative curve into two distinct coordinates, which helps, yet stating the exact balance point in advance, as a number for a given system, has still been done for almost none of them.

**It was built by one kind of mind.** Chapter 8 predicts that an integrated mind produces a coherent whole and overlooks exceptions. The reader should assume that this has happened here, and that people who know each field better will find where.

## What the theory asks of us

If the theory survives its tests, one piece of practical advice follows from it, and it has appeared in every part of the book.

*Share code, not beliefs.*

A collective becomes more intelligent when its members understand one another better: when they share a language, a notation, a way of stating a claim so that it can be checked. It becomes less intelligent when they come to hold the same opinions, however true those opinions are, because it loses the independent errors that let it correct itself. The bees arrived at this long before we did. They share the dance and keep their own judgement of the site.

The advice applies at every scale the book has visited. In a marriage, understand each other perfectly and go on disagreeing. In a team, invest in common ground and protect the dissenter. In science, standardise the methods and diversify the hypotheses. In a society, teach a common language and distrust unanimity. In building artificial minds, prefer the translator to the oracle, and many models to one.

And in reading this book: take its definitions, its measures and its predictions, which are offered as a common language for a subject that has lacked one. Do not take its conclusions on trust. Go and inspect the site yourself. Then come back and dance.
