# Chapter 18. An intelligent Schrödinger's cat

*This chapter is the most speculative in the book. It takes the theory's idea of an agent to the one part of physics where the role of the observer has never been settled. Readers who want only the well-supported parts of the theory can skip it without loss. Readers who enjoy the edge should stay.*

## The cat, and the friend who looked

In 1935 Erwin Schrödinger described a cat shut in a steel box with a tiny amount of radioactive material, a detector, and a flask of poison that the detector would break if a single atom decayed. Quantum theory says that after an hour the atom is in a combination of two states, decayed and not decayed. If the theory is applied to everything in the box, the cat is then in a combination of dead and alive, until someone opens the lid (1). Schrödinger meant it as a demonstration that something was wrong. Nobody has ever seen a combination of a dead and a living cat.

In 1961 the physicist Eugene Wigner sharpened the problem by putting a person in the box (2). Suppose a friend of his performs a measurement inside a sealed laboratory. The friend sees a definite result: the pointer went left, say. Wigner, outside, has not looked. If he applies quantum theory to the laboratory as a whole, friend included, he must describe it as a combination of "friend saw left" and "friend saw right". When did the result become a fact: when the friend saw it, or when Wigner opened the door?

Wigner's own answer, at the time, was that consciousness makes the difference. A conscious observer cannot be in a combination of two states of knowledge, so the friend's seeing must have settled it. On that view minds have a special physical power: they make facts.

That is the idea this chapter begins from, because it was the starting intuition behind this part of the theory: that intelligence might be what turns quantum possibility into fact. The version the theory ended up proposing is different, and stranger. Both versions are set out below, with an experiment that could tell them apart.

## The problem, plainly

Quantum theory has two rules for how things change. While a system is left alone, its state evolves smoothly and predictably, and may come to include many possibilities at once. When the system is measured, one possibility is found, with a probability the theory gives exactly. The theory is silent on what counts as a measurement. Is it a detector clicking? A record being written? A person becoming aware?

For practical purposes it does not matter. Any interaction with a large, warm object spreads the information about a quantum system into its surroundings so quickly that the many possibilities can no longer be made to interfere, a process called decoherence. But decoherence does not say which possibility is the real one, or for whom. The question of when a possibility becomes a fact is still open, and Wigner's friend is the cleanest way of asking it.

## Facts for someone

The theory of this book has spent fourteen chapters defining an agent: a bounded system with memory, inputs, a processing rule and outputs, which extracts information from the world and uses it. It is natural to ask whether that definition can do any work here.

The proposal is this. **A fact becomes actual for an agent when that agent has extracted usable information about it. For any agent that has not, the matter remains open, and open in the full quantum sense, not merely unknown.**

Call this relative actuality. For the friend, who has seen the pointer, the result is a fact. For Wigner, who has not, the laboratory really is in a combination of both results, and experiments he could in principle perform on it would show interference between them. Neither is mistaken. There is no view from nowhere that settles which description is the true one. "Has it happened?" is an incomplete question, like "is it to the left?". The complete question is "has it happened for whom?".

This is not a new kind of idea in physics. In 1957 Hugh Everett proposed that quantum states are relative to the state of the observer (3). In 1996 Carlo Rovelli argued that all quantum facts are relations between systems, with no absolute state of anything (4). What the theory of this book adds is a specific account of who the "someone" is, and of how much.

**It says who.** The systems to which facts are relative are agents, as defined in Chapter 3: things with a memory that persists and a boundary across which information is drawn. A stray air molecule that bounces off the cat carries away information, and contributes to decoherence. It does not keep a record or use it. It is not an agent, and no fact is actual *for it*.

**It says how much.** Extraction of information comes in degrees. An agent may learn everything about an event, or half, or a faint hint. So actuality should come in degrees too. A simple measure is the fraction of the information about an event that the agent has acquired: zero when the agent knows nothing, one when the agent's record fully determines what happened. In between, the event is partly settled for that agent. Experiments on weak measurement, in which a little information is extracted and a little interference is lost, already show this trade-off in the laboratory. The theory's addition is to read the trade-off as a degree of actuality.

**It applies to minds inside minds.** This is the consequence that connects the idea to the rest of the book. Agents nest. A neuron, a person and a society are agents at different levels, and Chapter 8 described the funnel through which each level learns only a summary of what the level below knows. So a fact can be actual at one level and not yet at another. A photon absorbed in your retina is a fact for a cell before it is a fact for you. An observation made by one scientist is a fact for her before it is a fact for science. Classically this is a truism about who has heard the news. If relative actuality is right, it is also a statement about physics: **facts become actual one agent, and one level, at a time.**

## Why we nevertheless agree

If facts are relative to agents, why does the world look the same to all of us? Why is there one moon and not eight billion private ones?

Part of the answer is a theorem. Quantum information cannot be copied: an unknown quantum state cannot be duplicated, or broadcast to several receivers (5). Classical information can be copied without limit. So whatever many agents hold in common must be classical. The shared world is classical because sharing requires copying.

The other part is an observation about which information gets copied. Wojciech Zurek has shown that the environment does not record everything about a quantum system equally. Certain properties, such as the position of a large object, are imprinted many times over in the light and air around it, while others are scrambled. Any observer who intercepts a small part of the environment can read those properties, and all observers who do so will agree. He calls this quantum Darwinism: the facts we share are the ones that survive and multiply (6).

Readers of Chapter 6 will recognise the shape of this. A collective can only build on what its members hold in common, and common ground has to be built by copying. Objectivity, on this view, is the largest shared context there is: the set of facts that have been copied into so many places that every agent draws on the same record. The classical world is the consensus of everything that has looked.

## The rival

Relative actuality has to be set beside the idea it grew from, because the two make different predictions.

The rival says that there is a real, physical event of collapse, the same for everyone, and that it is triggered when a sufficiently capable agent extracts information. On this view the friend's seeing settles the matter absolutely. Wigner's description of the laboratory as a combination of both results is then simply wrong, and an experiment designed to show interference between them would fail.

Under relative actuality, the experiment would succeed, provided Wigner could keep the laboratory isolated and under control. The friend's fact would be real for the friend and would leave the laboratory, from outside, still open.

The rival view makes minds special in the most direct way possible. Relative actuality makes them special in a quieter way: as the things for which there are facts.

## An experiment with a dial

For sixty years Wigner's friend was a thought experiment. It is now becoming a real one.

In 2018 Časlav Brukner proposed an extended version, with two laboratories, two friends and two outside observers sharing entangled particles (7). In 2020 a group led by Howard Wiseman, Eric Cavalcanti and Geoff Pryde proved a theorem about it. If three assumptions hold together, that an observed event is an absolute fact for everyone, that the experimenters' choices are free, and that influences do not travel faster than light, then the statistics of the experiment must satisfy certain inequalities. Quantum theory predicts that the inequalities are violated. The authors named the conjunction of assumptions Local Friendliness, and they performed the experiment in a simple form, in which the role of the "friend" was played by the path of a single photon. The inequalities were violated, as quantum theory said they would be (8).

The obvious objection is that the path of a photon is not an observer. Nobody doubts that a photon can be in a combination of states. The authors acknowledged it and pointed to the real test: a friend that is unquestionably an observer and yet can be isolated and controlled as a quantum system. No human being qualifies. In 2023 Wiseman, Cavalcanti and Eleanor Rieffel described what would: an artificial intelligence of human-level ability running on a large quantum computer, whose every step could in principle be reversed. They estimated how hard the experiment would be, and concluded that it lies far beyond present technology and is not forbidden by any law (9). Their argument has been examined critically, including the point that the assumptions may need strengthening (10).

The theory of this book suggests a way to make that distant experiment into a programme that can start much sooner. Between a photon and a human-level intelligence there is not a gap but a scale, and this book has spent its length describing it. An agent can have one bit of memory or a billion. It can extract a hundredth of the available information or all of it. It can process what it extracts a little or a lot. Each of these is a dial.

So do not ask the yes-or-no question, "can an observer be put in a combination of states?". Run the extended experiment with friends of increasing capability, one notch at a time: a single quantum bit that records the result, then a small circuit that records and acts on it, then a larger one that learns from it. At each setting, measure whether the inequalities are still violated.

The two views now predict different curves.

- **If relative actuality is right**, the violation persists at every setting, for as long as the friend can be kept isolated. There is no threshold. Agents of any size have their facts, and none of those facts is absolute.
- **If the rival is right**, the violation should weaken and vanish above some level of capability. That level would be a new constant of nature: the amount of information-processing at which possibility becomes fact for everyone.

There is a tension here with the book's own earlier chapters that must be stated plainly. Chapter 2 showed that an agent pays Landauer's price in heat whenever it erases or overwrites memory, and Chapter 3's agents are all systems that do so continually. Yet Wigner can reverse the friend coherently only if the friend leaks *zero* heat to the outside environment. A purely reversible quantum circuit, one that stores a record in clean quantum memory without dissipating heat, differs little in standard quantum mechanics from a stray molecule entangling with the cat, so standard quantum theory predicts the violation will persist at every dial setting unless a new objective-collapse law intervenes. A truly dissipative agent, by contrast, leaks heat into a thermal bath and cannot be reversed for ordinary thermodynamic reasons. Running the dial across both reversible and weakly dissipative circuits is what would separate ordinary decoherence from any genuine agent threshold.

Either result would be among the most important in the history of physics. The first would establish that facts are relative even for thinking things. The second would locate, in the laboratory, the point at which a mind makes the world definite, and it would give the word "agent" a physical meaning sharper than any in this book.

## What follows if it is right

Return for a moment to the hierarchy of minds, and suppose that relative actuality holds.

Then each agent lives in a world of facts that are facts for it. Your world is the set of events about which you hold usable information. Humanity's world is the much smaller set that has passed through the funnel into the common record. Most of what is actual for you, the exact shade of the sky this morning, is not actual for humanity and never will be. And if there are larger and slower minds of the kind Chapter 16 imagined, then most of what is actual for humanity, a century's worth of it, may not yet be actual for them.

The last provocation in the preface said this: what is real for you may not yet be real for the larger thing you are part of. It can now be read exactly. In a hierarchy of minds there is no single moment at which a thing becomes true. Truth arrives level by level, as each agent in turn takes in the news.

## In short

- Quantum theory does not say when a possibility becomes a fact. Wigner's friend poses the question sharply: a result is definite for the friend who saw it, while the laboratory remains undecided for an observer outside.
- The theory's proposal, relative actuality: a fact is actual for an agent when that agent has extracted usable information about it, and for other agents the matter remains open in the full quantum sense. This is a speculation in the family of relational interpretations.
- What the theory adds: the "someone" is an agent as defined in this book; actuality comes in degrees, as the fraction of the available information extracted; and in a hierarchy of agents, facts become actual one level at a time.
- The shared world is classical because only classical information can be copied to many agents, and because the environment copies some facts abundantly.
- The rival view holds that capable agents cause an absolute collapse.
- Experiments of the extended Wigner's-friend type can distinguish the two. The theory proposes running them with friends of increasing capability and looking for a threshold.

## How we could be wrong

This chapter could be wrong in more ways than any other. If the extended experiment, run with increasingly capable friends, shows the violation disappearing at some threshold, relative actuality is refuted and the rival is supported. If quantum theory itself fails for large or complex systems, as theories of spontaneous collapse propose, then neither view is needed and facts become absolute for reasons that have nothing to do with agents. The proposed measure of graded actuality may not be the right one, and it leaves undefined what makes information "usable". The identification of the systems to which facts are relative with agents, and not with any physical system whatever, as other relational accounts hold, may be an unnecessary addition that no experiment can detect. If that is so, the chapter adds a vocabulary and no physics. And the whole proposal inherits every difficulty of the relational family of interpretations, including the question of how different agents' facts are guaranteed to agree when they compare notes.
