# Chapter 9. How a mind divides

## A ring around a plateau

The greenish warbler is a small, olive-coloured songbird of the Asian forests. Its populations form a ring around the treeless Tibetan plateau. Walk around the ring and the birds change gradually: each population sings a little differently from its neighbours, and neighbours still recognise each other and interbreed.

But the two ends of the ring meet again in central Siberia, and there the two forms live side by side without interbreeding. When researchers played recordings of one form's song to the other, the birds did not respond. They no longer recognised it as the song of their own kind (1, 2). The biologists who mapped the ring concluded that it shows, spread out across space, what normally happens over time: one species becoming two.

Nothing killed the channel between the two ends of the ring. No mountain rose between them, and they could meet whenever they liked. The code drifted, one small step per population, until the message could no longer be read. Two birds sit on the same branch and cannot hear each other.

This chapter is about what that means. Part III has so far asked how minds stack inside one another. Here we ask the opposite question: what holds a mind together, and what makes it come apart?

## Three ways to be one

The book has already met this question twice without naming it.

Chapter 4 asked when a collective can *act* as one, and answered with the coherence number: signals must cross the collective faster than the world it is dealing with changes. A batter's brain can act as one on a fastball. A swarm cannot.

Chapter 6 asked when two people are close to being *one system*, and pointed ahead to Chapter 12's answer: a self ends where the traffic across its boundary becomes much thinner than the traffic within. Chapter 4 called this ratio *outside against inside*.

The warblers raise a third version: when does a collective stay *one kind*, able to read its own members? The answer has the same form. Its parts must exchange messages faster than their codes drift apart.

Put the three side by side.

| To be one in this sense | the traffic inside must outrun | Example of failure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Act as one | the pace of the world | a swarm trying to catch a ball |
| Be a self | the traffic across the boundary | a nation, whose borders carry about as much traffic as its interior |
| Stay one kind | the drift of the shared code | the two warblers in Siberia |

This is the theory's claim, and it is a strong one: **unity is a ratio of rates.** Whether something counts as one mind is not a matter of what it is made of, or of where the lines are drawn on a map. It is a matter of how fast information moves inside it compared with three other speeds. One law, applied three times. The rest of this chapter follows the third application, because it is the one that runs across the whole history of life.

## Every species is a mind

Ask a biologist what a species is, and the standard answer is: a group of organisms that can interbreed. Look at that definition with this book's eyes. Breeding is an exchange of information. Each parent contributes a genome, and the offspring is a new combination of the two. A species is the set of organisms among whom that exchange can happen. In other words, a species is defined by a communication channel, and its boundary is where the channel closes. That is exactly how Chapter 3 defined a collective.

It has the other ingredients too. Its agents are organisms. Its memory is the gene pool. And it learns. Natural selection writes information about the environment into that memory, generation after generation. The geneticist Motoo Kimura first tried to measure the rate in 1961 and arrived at a fraction of a bit per generation (3). Later arguments put the ceiling at around one bit per generation (4), and recent work has proved a general limit on how much information selection can accumulate each generation (5).

So a species is a mind of the federated kind, and an almost unimaginably slow one. Its tick is a generation. Its learning rate is of the order of a bit per tick. A person behaves at ten bits per second. A species thinking through its genes takes years or decades to acquire what you acquire in a blink. But it never forgets, it is spread over a continent, and it can run for a million years. Like the swarm, it trades speed for reach, and like the swarm it has no self. This is a claim of the theory. The measurements of evolution's information rate are established science.

### A second, faster channel

Many species also talk in the ordinary sense, and that gives the species-mind a faster line.

The best example is in the Pacific. Male humpback whales sing, and all the males in a population sing the current version of the same song. Researchers recording six neighbouring populations across the South Pacific over eleven years found that new songs appear in the west, off Australia, and sweep eastward from population to population, like cultural ripples, reaching French Polynesia about two years later (6, 7). It is a conversation the width of an ocean, in which a single message takes two years to cross. Chapter 10 would call two years the crossing time of that mind, and nobody inside it hears more than a local stretch of the wave.

## Why codes drift

Darwin showed where species come from. A population is separated from the rest for long enough, it changes, and it becomes a species of its own. In this book's language, that is a mind dividing in two, and the theory says exactly why it happens.

Chapter 6 argued that shared context is a store that has to be built by communication. It also has to be *maintained* by communication. Two groups that stop exchanging messages do not keep the same code for ever. Each drifts. When they have drifted far enough, they can no longer decode each other, and then the channel cannot be reopened even if the barrier between them disappears. That is the warblers' story: the species boundary *is* a failure of decoding.

Darwin himself noticed that the same thing happens to human languages. In *The Descent of Man* he wrote that the formation of different languages and of distinct species are "curiously parallel" (8, 9). The theory's claim is that this is not a parallel. It is one process seen twice. Latin, separated into regions that seldom spoke to each other, became French, Spanish and Romanian. An ancestral warbler, stretched around a plateau, became two birds. In both cases a collective held together by shared code split when traffic fell too low to keep the code shared.

That gives a pure number, to add to the list from Chapter 4: the rate at which a collective's parts exchange messages, divided by the rate at which their codes drift. While the ratio is high, the collective stays one. When it falls too low, it divides, and the division is permanent. Population geneticists have a famous rule of thumb of this kind: roughly one migrant per generation between two populations is enough to stop them drifting apart (10). The theory predicts that an equivalent threshold exists for every kind of collective: for dialects, for scientific fields that stop reading each other, for the divisions of a company.

Two features of the rule deserve attention.

**It is asymmetric in time.** Drift is slow and quiet, and a divided pair can stay in contact for a long while after the critical point has passed. Reunion is not the reverse of separation. Once the codes have parted, renewed contact does not restore them, because the parties no longer share the context they would need to rebuild it. A collective can lose its unity through centuries of quiet and never get it back.

**It sets a size limit that has nothing to do with the speed of light.** Chapter 13 will find that a mind which must act as one cannot be larger than light travels in one of its thoughts. The drift rule gives a second, gentler limit for minds that only need to stay one kind. Its radius is however far a message can travel before the code drifts. For genes that radius is set by how far organisms wander in a generation; for languages, by how far words travel in a few centuries. Chapter 13 applies it to a species spreading between the stars.

## The family tree of minds

Put these pieces together and the living world looks different. The tree of life is a family tree of minds. Some four billion years ago there was one, sharing one code. It has been dividing ever since, each division a channel that closed and never reopened. Today there are millions of them on this planet, each slowly learning about its own corner of the world, almost none able to read another's signals. They are the purest case of Chapter 10's minds that cannot see each other, and we walk among them every day.

They are not wholly cut off from one another, though. Chapter 6 pointed out that the deepest layer of shared context, the genetic code itself, is common to every living thing. That layer is the last trace of the time when all these minds were one. It is why a virus can read a human cell, and why a gene from a jellyfish works in a mouse.

And it makes plain what is unusual about us. Every species has the slow channel of the genes. A few have a faster one in song or scent. Humans built a third, language, and a fourth, writing, each many orders of magnitude faster than the last. Our species-mind does not run at a bit per generation. It runs at the speed of speech, multiplied by eight billion. The human takeover of the planet, on this view, was not the victory of a clever animal over stupid ones. It was the victory of a fast collective mind over slow ones.

The faster channels did not abolish drift, however. They moved it. Our genes are still one code, but our languages number in the thousands, because speech drifts far faster than DNA. Humanity is one species and many conversations. Chapter 13 shows that those conversations are the organs of whatever mind humanity is.

## In short

- Unity is a ratio of rates. A collective acts as one while its internal signals outrun the world, is a self while its internal traffic outruns its boundary traffic, and stays one kind while its traffic outruns the drift of its code. This is the theory's claim.
- Every species is a collective mind by this book's definition: organisms exchanging information through genes, learning at about a bit per generation. Some have faster channels too, such as whale song crossing an ocean in two years.
- Species form when communication falls too low to keep a code shared. The greenish warbler shows the code drifting until it cannot be read. Languages split the same way, as Darwin noticed.
- The ratio of exchange to drift is a new pure number. Roughly one migrant per generation holds populations together; the theory predicts similar thresholds for dialects, fields and organisations.
- Division is asymmetric: once codes have parted, contact does not restore them.
- The tree of life is a family tree of minds, still joined by the genetic code. Humanity's faster channels made it a fast collective mind, and gave it many languages.

## How we could be wrong

The claim that a species is a mind would be empty if it predicted nothing. It predicts that species boundaries should coincide with failures of signal recognition, as in the warbler, more often than with physical inability to breed. It predicts that a threshold ratio of exchange to drift governs splitting in languages and organisations as it does in genes, and that once crossed, the split does not heal on renewed contact. If collectives routinely stay unified with almost no traffic, split despite heavy traffic, or reunite easily after their codes have parted, the claim is wrong. The broader claim, that the three kinds of unity are one law, would fail if the three ratios turned out to have unrelated thresholds that no common account can explain.
