# Chapter 7. How much can a mind do?

## Nine hours of chewing

A wild gorilla spends most of its waking day eating. Leaves, stems and fruit are bulky and poor in calories, and an adult male weighing as much as two men has to keep chewing to stay alive.

In 2012 two Brazilian neuroscientists, Karina Fonseca-Azevedo and Suzana Herculano-Houzel, asked why such a large animal has a much smaller brain than ours. Their answer was arithmetic (1). Neurons are expensive, and the cost is the same for every one of them: across mammals, the brain's energy use turns out to be close to a fixed amount per neuron, whatever the species or the size of the brain (2). So every extra billion neurons has to be paid for with extra food, and food on a raw diet costs time. They calculated that a primate eating raw food for a punishing ten hours a day could afford at most about 113 billion neurons, and then only with a body no heavier than about 64 kilograms (3). Gorillas already eat for close to nine hours. A human brain, fed the gorilla's way, would need more than nine hours of eating every day (4).

Their explanation of how our ancestors escaped this limit is cooking. Cooked food yields far more energy for far less chewing, and they argue that the shift to a cooked diet, probably by *Homo erectus*, is what made a brain of 86 billion neurons affordable (1).

Whether or not that account of human history holds, the arithmetic behind it is the subject of this chapter. A mind's size is not free. It is bought with energy, and energy is bought with time, food, wiring and heat. The question of how much a mind can do is a question about its budget.

## The other half of the formula

Chapter 5 defined intelligence as efficiency: how much a mind achieves per bit of information it uses. That is one half of the picture. A mind that wastes nothing but can handle only one bit a minute is perfect and useless, like a flawless accountant who adds one number a day.

The other half is **capacity**: how many bits per second a mind can take in, process and pass on. What a mind actually accomplishes in a given time is the product of the two:

**intelligent output = capacity × efficiency**

Efficiency, Chapter 5 showed, has a ceiling of one. This chapter asks whether capacity has a ceiling too, and what sets it. The answer, in brief, is that three things do: the energy each bit costs, the wiring that carries bits from place to place, and the time those bits take to arrive. Whichever is tightest sets the limit. And all three get tighter in a particular way as a mind grows, which turns out to decide what kind of mind it can be.

## The price of a spike

Start with energy. Chapter 2 estimated that the brain spends about ten million times more energy per event than the thermodynamic minimum. Where does it go?

Mostly into talking. In 2001 David Attwell and Simon Laughlin worked out an energy budget for the grey matter of the brain, process by process, from the sodium ions that flood into a neuron when it fires to the pumps that must push them out again. Their central finding was that energy use depends strongly on how often neurons fire (5). A later update found that about half of the cortex's signalling energy is spent at the receiving end of synapses, a fifth on the spikes themselves, and a fifth on keeping neurons ready to fire (6). The brain's bill is, overwhelmingly, the cost of communication between its agents.

That bill sets a hard limit on how much of the brain can be busy at once. In 2003 Peter Lennie took the known energy consumption of the human cortex and divided it by the cost of a single spike. The result was that fewer than one percent of cortical neurons can be strongly active at the same time (7). You may have heard that people use only ten percent of their brains. The truth is stranger. You can afford to use only about one percent of it at any moment, and if every neuron fired flat out together the brain could not pay for it. Concentrated, synchronous firing of the kind the budget forbids is not a higher state of thought. Chapter 3 met it under its medical name: a seizure.

So the brain must be sparse. Most of its neurons are quiet most of the time, and what matters is which few are speaking.

## Why thin channels win

The most important measurement in this chapter comes from the eye of a fly.

In 1998 Simon Laughlin, Rob de Ruyter van Steveninck and John Anderson measured both the information carried by neurons in the blowfly's retina and the energy those neurons used to carry it. One bit passed across a chemical synapse cost about ten thousand molecules of ATP, the cell's energy currency. One bit carried by a graded signal in a photoreceptor or an interneuron, or by spikes, cost between a million and ten million (8). Every figure was many orders of magnitude above the thermodynamic floor.

The decisive finding was in the comparison. The cost of a bit was not fixed. It rose with the rate. A channel that carried few bits per second carried each of them more cheaply than a channel that carried many. A later study comparing the photoreceptors of several fly species found the same pattern: the cells built for the highest information rates paid the most for each bit, and the blowfly's fast photoreceptors used about ten times more energy to code the same amount of information (9). The authors of the 1998 study drew the conclusion themselves: because a weak, low-capacity pathway transmits information more economically, the economics favour spreading information across many pathways rather than concentrating it in one (8). A theoretical analysis reached the matching result from the other side: a system that must pay for its bits does best, over the long run, by sending them at a modest rate rather than as fast as it can (10).

Stop and look at what that means for this book. If each channel's cost per bit rises with its speed, then a hundred slow channels carry the same information as one fast channel for less energy. A mind under an energy budget should therefore not be one fast agent. It should be many slow ones, each sending a trickle.

This is the theory's claim, and it may be the most important one in Part II: **the "many" in "many minds" is not an accident of history. It is what energy efficiency looks like.** Nature builds minds out of crowds of thin, slow, cheap channels because that is the cheapest way to move bits. The bees' seven-bit dance and the neuron's sparse spikes are not failures to build a better wire. They are the economical solution, and they appear wherever information has to be paid for.

## The price of size

Energy says: use many thin channels. Geometry then says what happens as the number of channels grows.

Chapter 4 showed the first part of the problem. In a mind whose agents talk mainly to their neighbours, computing grows with the volume and cross-talk grows only with the area of a cut through the middle. Time makes it worse. Signals travel at a fixed speed, so the crossing time grows with the width of the mind.

Take a mind and make it ten times larger in volume, at the same density of agents. It has ten times as many agents and ten times the computing. Without long cables, the traffic across its middle grows only about four and a half times, and a signal takes about twice as long to cross it. Each part does more thinking relative to what it can tell the rest, and each message arrives later. (These ratios follow from the geometry alone; they are our arithmetic, not a measurement.)

Brains fight this by laying down cables, and Chapter 4 showed the price: across mammals, white matter grows as the 4/3 power of grey matter (11). But cables only buy bandwidth. They cannot buy speed, because a thicker, faster axon takes more room and more energy, and most long-range fibres in large brains are thin (12).

In 1994 James Ringo and his colleagues worked out what this means for a human brain. For an average-sized fibre linking the two temporal lobes, a signal takes over 25 milliseconds to get from one hemisphere to the other, one way (12). That is comparable to the brain's whole crossing time. They proposed that this delay is why the two hemispheres specialise: tasks that need fast, tight collaboration between neurons are best kept on one side, where the wires are short. And they made a prediction from it: the larger brains of elephants and whales should be even more divided into specialised halves (12). The conjecture is contested. Later modelling argued that their results show a fixed delay before the hemispheres start exchanging signals, not that delay causes specialisation (13). But the physics behind it is not in dispute: a bigger brain is a slower brain across its own width.

Put energy and geometry together and a general conclusion follows, again as a claim of the theory. **As a mind grows, the capacity of its parts grows in proportion to their number, but the capacity of the whole to take part in a single thought grows more slowly.** Beyond some size it stops paying to keep every part in close conversation with every other. The economical design becomes a set of modules that each think a great deal and say little to one another.

That is the threshold between two kinds of mind, and Chapter 8 is about what lies on either side of it.

## What the machines spend

Our own machines face the same arithmetic, with different prices.

Frontier, one of the world's leading supercomputers, was the first to pass a billion billion operations per second in a standard benchmark. It did so while drawing about 21 megawatts (14). That is the power of a million human brains. Counting very roughly, a brain gets between a hundred and a thousand times more of its basic events out of each joule than Frontier gets operations, though a synaptic event and a floating-point operation are not the same kind of thing, and the comparison is only a sense of scale (our estimate).

The machines make up for that by sheer scale. In 2024 the world's data centres used about 415 terawatt-hours of electricity, roughly 1.5 percent of all the electricity generated on Earth, and the International Energy Agency expects that figure to more than double by 2030 (15). In 2025 alone, data-centre electricity use rose by 17 percent (16). Averaged over the year, 415 terawatt-hours is a continuous draw of about 47 gigawatts. All eight billion human brains together, at about 20 watts each, draw about 160 gigawatts. The machines already consume something like a third as much power as every human brain on the planet, and the gap is closing fast (both figures are our arithmetic).

Seen through this chapter, the difference between brains and machines is a difference in what each was forced to economise. Evolution priced energy dearly: every neuron had to be paid for in hours of chewing. So brains were pushed toward high efficiency and modest capacity, the profile Chapter 5 found in children, who learn language from a tiny fraction of the data a machine needs. Machines grew up where energy was cheap and abundant, so they were pushed the other way, toward enormous capacity and low efficiency.

That suggests a prediction. As the energy bill becomes the binding constraint on machines, as the figures above suggest it is becoming, the theory expects machine designs to drift toward the brain's answers: sparse activity, in which only a small fraction of the system works on any given input; many slow, cheap channels instead of a few fast ones; and more effort spent on priors, so that less has to be learned from data. In the terms of this book, machines will be pushed from capacity toward efficiency by the same price that pushed brains.

## The most intelligent kilogram

Does capacity have an absolute ceiling, as efficiency does? It does, and physics can say where it is.

The speed at which any physical system can change from one distinguishable state to another is limited by its energy. Norman Margolus and Lev Levitin proved the bound in 1998 (17). In 2000 Seth Lloyd used it, together with the laws of thermodynamics, to work out the computing power of an "ultimate laptop": a computer with a mass of one kilogram in a volume of one litre, pushed to the limits of physics (18). His answer was about 5 × 10⁵⁰ operations per second, on a memory of about 10³¹ bits. To an outside observer it would look like a plasma at around a billion degrees. In the paper's own image, it would resemble a small piece of the Big Bang (18).

Combine that with Chapter 5. Efficiency can be at most one: one bit of the world settled for each bit used. Capacity can be at most Lloyd's limit for a given mass and energy. Multiply them, and there is a definite maximum to how much intelligence any kilogram of matter can deliver per second. It is the theory's claim, but it rests on two proved bounds: **there is a most intelligent object of any given size, and physics already knows its specification.**

There is a twist, and it is the theme of this book in miniature. The ultimate laptop is ten centimetres across. Light takes about a third of a nanosecond to cross it. In that time, by our arithmetic from Lloyd's figures, each of its bits could change state some ten billion times. Even the most intelligent kilogram in the universe could not think as one unit. Its parts would have to work mostly alone, exchanging summaries now and then, like neurons, like bees. The most powerful mind physics allows would still be a crowd.

## Capacity is not intelligence

It is easy to mistake capacity for intelligence, because capacity is what we can most easily see. A supercomputer's speed is printed on a plaque; a brain's efficiency is hidden in what it does not need to be told.

The formula keeps them apart. A mind with vast capacity and low efficiency spends torrents of bits to achieve what a frugal mind does with a trickle. A mind with perfect efficiency and tiny capacity is a thermostat: it wastes nothing and does almost nothing. What matters is the product. And the two factors are not independent. Energy prices push them against each other, and every real mind, evolved or built, sits somewhere on a trade-off between doing a lot and needing little.

Part II is now complete. A mind made of agents can be described with a clock and a ruler (Chapter 4), an efficiency (Chapter 5), a measure of meaning (Chapter 6) and a capacity (this chapter). Each has a physical limit. Part III uses them to climb from one level of mind to the next.

## In short

- Intelligent output is capacity times efficiency. Efficiency has a ceiling of one; capacity is limited by energy, wiring and time.
- The brain's energy use is close to a fixed amount per neuron, and most of it pays for communication. Brain size is therefore an energy budget. On a raw diet, primates are limited to roughly as many neurons as the hours of feeding allow; cooking may have lifted that limit for our ancestors.
- Spikes are so expensive that fewer than one percent of cortical neurons can be strongly active at once. A brain must be sparse.
- The cost of each bit rises with the rate at which a channel sends. Many slow channels are cheaper than one fast one. The theory's claim: this is why minds are built from many agents.
- As a mind grows, its parts' capacity grows with their number but the whole's capacity to think as one grows more slowly, because cross-talk scales with area and delays with width. Beyond some size, modules that say little to each other become the economical design. In the human brain, signals between hemispheres take over 25 milliseconds.
- Machines have far greater capacity than brains and far lower efficiency, because energy was cheap for them. Data centres already draw about a third as much power as all human brains together, by our estimate. The theory predicts that rising energy costs will push machine designs toward the brain's solutions.
- Physics sets an absolute maximum for capacity per kilogram. Combined with the ceiling on efficiency, there is a most intelligent object of a given size. Even it would have to be a collective.

## How we could be wrong

The claim that energy favours many thin channels depends on the cost per bit rising with rate in general, not only in the fly retina and the systems studied so far. If high-rate channels turned out, in other nervous systems or in engineered ones, to be as cheap per bit as slow ones, the energetic argument for plurality would fail and the "many" would need another explanation. The claim that integrated capacity grows more slowly than size predicts that larger brains are more modular and that very large brains, such as those of elephants and whales, are more specialised between hemispheres. Evidence that large brains are as uniformly integrated as small ones would count against it. The prediction about machines can be checked within a decade: if machine designs under rising energy prices do not become sparser and more frugal with data, the theory has misjudged the forces acting on them. The bounds on capacity per kilogram are established physics; the claim that they combine with the efficiency bound into a ceiling on intelligence holds only if Chapter 5's definition of efficiency is the right one.
