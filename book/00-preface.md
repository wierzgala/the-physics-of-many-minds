# Preface

This book follows one idea wherever it leads. Intelligence is not a substance hidden inside a skull. It is a physical process that appears whenever many agents store information, work on it, and pass it to one another.

A brain is such a process, built from neurons. A honeybee swarm is another, built from bees. There may be others that we have not yet learned to recognise, because we have been looking for minds in the wrong places and measuring them with the wrong rulers.

The intuition is not new. Writers have compared ant colonies to brains for decades, and Marvin Minsky described the mind as a "society" of simpler agents. What this book adds is an insistence on physics. We will count information in bits, distances in metres, time in seconds and energy in joules. Then we will ask what those numbers allow and what they forbid.

## How to read this book

The book is the slow, descriptive companion to the technical foundations document. That document states the theory in compressed form: assumptions, variables, equations and predictions. Here the same ideas are built up from things you can see: a swarm hanging from a branch, a slime mould that redraws a railway map, a crowd guessing the weight of an ox.

Three kinds of statement appear throughout, and we try never to blur them:

- **Established findings**: results from experiments and observations, with sources in the notes.
- **The theory's claims**: hypotheses that follow from the framework and still need testing.
- **Speculation**: ideas at the edge, marked as such.

Each chapter ends with two short sections. *In short* states what the chapter claims. *How we could be wrong* names the observation that would undermine it. A theory that cannot say how it could fail is not yet a theory.
