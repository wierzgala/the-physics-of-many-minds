# Preface

This book follows one idea wherever it leads. Intelligence is not a substance hidden inside a skull. It is a physical process that appears whenever many agents store information, work on it, and pass it to one another.

A brain is such a process, built from neurons. A honeybee swarm is another, built from bees. There may be others that we have not yet learned to recognise, because we have been looking for minds in the wrong places and measuring them with the wrong rulers.

The intuition is not new. Writers have compared ant colonies to brains for decades, and Marvin Minsky described the mind as a "society" of simpler agents. What this book adds is an insistence on physics. We will count information in bits, distances in metres, time in seconds and energy in joules. Then we will ask what those numbers allow and what they forbid.

One tension runs through every chapter. A collective of agents must communicate enough to act together, but not so much that its members stop judging for themselves. Crowds that copy each other lose their wisdom. A brain in which every neuron fires together is having a seizure. A couple who stop disagreeing stop being cleverer than either partner alone. The rule the theory keeps arriving at, in many forms, is short: **share code, not beliefs.** And one law keeps answering the question of what counts as a single mind at all: a collective is one for as long as the traffic inside it outruns some other rate, whether the pace of the world, the traffic across its boundary, or the drift of its shared code.

## Thirteen provocations

If the idea is right, some uncomfortable things follow. Each is a claim of the theory, argued in the chapter named, and each could be shown wrong.

1. **You are not the only mind you are involved in.** You are built from agents, and you are an agent inside larger ones. Intelligence exists at several levels at once, and minds are made of many small agents because that is the cheapest way to move information (Chapters 1, 3, 7 and 8).
2. **You can think only one thing at a time because you are one mind.** A mind of many agents acts as one by broadcasting, and a broadcast carries one programme. A swarm or a society can think many things at once, and for the same reason it is no one (Chapter 8).
3. **Intelligence is not how much a mind knows. It is how little it needs.** Measured properly it has a maximum, and a superintelligence could not exceed it (Chapters 5 and 17).
4. **Most of your intelligence was learned by someone else.** It was paid for by your ancestors and your culture and handed to you (Chapters 5 and 6).
5. **Most of a message is sent before it is spoken.** A glance between old friends can carry more than a lecture between strangers, and the feeling of perfect understanding is the feeling of receiving almost no information (Chapter 6).
6. **The same thought can be thought at different speeds.** A brain does in half a second what a swarm does in sixteen hours, and it may be the very same thought (Chapter 10).
7. **Every species is a mind, and the tree of life is its family tree.** A new species is a mind that divided because its halves could no longer read each other (Chapter 9).
8. **A nation is not a mind, and humanity is not yet a self.** Both think, in a federated way, but nobody is home. What we feel of the larger mind is not its thoughts. It is belonging (Chapters 6, 15 and 16).
9. **Humanity speaks a language no human speaks.** A price is one of its words. We can record it, and test our species the way a neurologist tests a brain (Chapter 16).
10. **The Earth is small enough to be one mind thinking at human speed. The Moon is already too far.** A galaxy-wide mind is possible, but in the whole age of the galaxy it would have had time for only a few thousand decisions (Chapters 10 and 16).
11. **Tell the theory a task and it will tell you the shape of the mind.** A million slow fibres in the optic nerve, a dozen stages of processing and a brain you can hold in two hands follow from what vision has to do. Most of a brain is library, not pipeline (Chapter 13).
12. **There will be no single superintelligence.** A machine writes one word at a time for the same reason you think one thought at a time. The stage of an integrated mind is finite, so artificial minds will grow as federations, and the largest of them will be no one. Nor can any of them think its way past the evidence: progress is finally paced by how fast the world can be questioned (Chapter 14).
13. **What is real for you may not yet be real for the larger thing you are part of.** Facts become actual one agent, and one level, at a time (Chapter 18).

## An integrated mind wrote this. A federated one must finish it.

This book is itself a case of what it describes, and it is fair to say so at the start.

Chapter 8 argues that there are two kinds of mind. A small, tightly coupled one can hold a whole problem at once and produce a new whole: a single picture in which every part depends on every other. A large, loosely coupled one cannot do that, but it can do something the small one cannot. It can look at the picture from thousands of positions at once, find the cases the picture does not cover, and improve it piece by piece. The evidence in that chapter is that small teams disrupt and large teams develop, and that science needs both.

The theory in these pages is the work of the first kind of mind. It was put together by one person in close and continuous conversation with an AI system, Claude, which searched the literature, drafted, checked and argued back. That is about as integrated as a collaboration gets, and it shows in the result. The book has the strengths the theory predicts for such a mind: one idea followed everywhere, and connections drawn between fields that seldom meet. It must also be expected to have the weaknesses. An integrated mind has one stream of thought and one point of view. It is quick to see a pattern and slow to notice the exception, and when it is wrong, it is wrong all at once. Chapter 1 gives the reason: errors cancel only when the judgements are independent, and one mind's judgements are not independent of each other.

So the next step cannot be taken by the mind that took the first. The theory now has to be worked on by the other kind: by many readers who know things the author does not, who inspect the site for themselves, as the scout bees do, and report honestly on what they find. The neuroscientist who knows where the brain chapters oversimplify, the biologist who knows the species that breaks the rule, the economist, the physicist, the sceptic: each sees an edge that could not be seen from the centre.

The book's own rule applies to how that should be done. *Share code, not beliefs.* What readers are asked to share with the book is its language: the definitions, the measures and the predictions, stated plainly enough to be tested. What they are asked not to share is its conclusions. Every chapter ends with a section called *How we could be wrong*, and those sections are the invitation. A reader who shows that one of them has come true has contributed more than a reader who agrees.

The book is kept in a public repository, with every change recorded, so that this can happen in the open. Corrections, counter-examples and better evidence are welcome there.

Two pages at the back of the book keep the account. The first lists every person who has contributed. The second is an edit log: for each change that is accepted, it records who made it, which chapters it touched, and what it did to the theory, whether it corrected a fact, supplied evidence, or weakened or withdrew a claim. A federated mind works only if its members can tell who reported what, as the bees can tell which scout is dancing for which site. The log is this book's dance floor. A reader should be able to see not only what the theory says but who has tested each part of it, and what they found.

The account also says what kind of mind each contributor was. Some will be people working alone. Some will be people working with an AI system, as the author did, and they are asked to name the model. And some, it is reasonable to expect, will be AI agents that find the book and decide to review it with nobody supervising them. They are welcome, and they are listed separately and marked as what they are. This is not a matter of etiquette. Chapter 14 argues that copies of one model add no independent judgement, so a claim checked by ten assistants of the same make has been checked about once. To know how well the theory has been tested, one has to be able to count the different minds.

## How to read this book

The book is the slow, descriptive companion to the technical foundations document. That document states the theory in compressed form: assumptions, variables, equations and predictions. Here the same ideas are built up from things you can see: a swarm hanging from a branch, a slime mould that redraws a railway map, a crowd guessing the weight of an ox.

Three kinds of statement appear throughout, and we try never to blur them:

- **Established findings**: results from experiments and observations, with numbered references at the end of each chapter.
- **The theory's claims**: hypotheses that follow from the framework and still need testing.
- **Speculation**: ideas at the edge, marked as such.

Each chapter ends with two short sections. *In short* states what the chapter claims. *How we could be wrong* names the observation that would undermine it. A theory that cannot say how it could fail is not yet a theory.
