# Chapter 13. Minds the size of worlds

## A country with two colours

In 2008 four researchers in Belgium were testing a new method for finding communities in very large networks. For data they used the records of a mobile phone operator: about two million customers, and who called whom. The method knew nothing about the customers. It only looked for groups of people who called each other more than they called outsiders (1).

When the groups were drawn as a map and coloured by the main language of their members, the country fell into two halves. One large cluster of communities was French-speaking and the other Dutch-speaking, with a single small mixed community between them. Belgium, one state with one government and one phone network, was two conversations.

Nobody told the algorithm about language. The boundary was simply there in the flow of information, as sharp as the boundary between two organs. This chapter is about what such boundaries mean. Neurons make a person. If the pattern continues, what do people make?

## Eight billion neurons

The obvious candidate is all of us. Run the numbers the way Part II taught.

- **Agents:** about 8.2 billion people (2).
- **What each takes in:** on the order of a billion bits per second through the senses (3).
- **What each passes on:** about ten bits per second of behaviour (3); about 39 bits per second when speaking (4).
- **Shared codes:** 7,164 living languages, of which just 23 cover more than half of humanity (5).

The ratio between what a person takes in and what a person gives out is about a hundred million. Chapter 8 showed that this ratio decides what kind of mind a collective can be. A brain is made of thin agents joined by thick wires: neurons do little alone, and nearly all the action is in the connections. Humanity is the reverse. It is made of thick agents joined by thin wires. Each of us holds a universe, and passes on a trickle.

That makes humanity a mind of the federated type, like the swarm: slow, wide, almost impossible to kill, and good at exactly one thing, which is pooling independent evidence from a great many places.

Does it actually think? The clearest evidence is science. Science has a shared code (mathematics, a working language, the conventions of the published paper). It has an external memory (the literature). It has independent agents who check claims for themselves, as bee scouts do. And it reaches decisions by a slow build-up of support that nobody declares and nobody can veto. No scientist decided that continents drift or that germs cause disease. The collective did, over decades, by a process that looks very much like dancing fading for the weaker sites.

## Organs made of language

A brain is not a uniform mass. It has areas, each densely wired within and sparsely wired to the others. If humanity is a mind, where are its areas?

Chapter 6 gives the answer in advance. Meaning flows where context is shared, and nothing concentrates shared context like a common language. The Belgian phone map is that prediction, confirmed before it was made.

At the global scale the picture is the same. A study that linked the world's languages through bilingual speakers and translated books found a network with one global hub, English, and a small set of intermediate hubs, including Spanish, German, French, Russian, Portuguese and Chinese (6). An idea born in a small language reaches the rest of humanity by passing through these hubs, much as a signal from one brain area reaches another through a few major tracts.

The modular structure continues downward as well. When researchers gathered data on the sizes of human groups, from closest confidants to bands and tribes, they found not a smooth spread but preferred sizes, each about three times the last: roughly 3 to 5, then 9 to 15, then 30 to 45, and onward (7). Society has levels, the way Chapter 11 found that brains do.

## Is a nation a mind?

Here the theory has to disappoint a great many flags.

A nation looks like a good candidate for an agent. It often has a language, and so high shared context. It has procedures for reaching decisions; an election is a quorum rule, like the fifteen bees at the nest box. It has a memory in its archives and laws.

But Chapter 12 set the test for being a *self*, not just a region of a larger system: what crosses the boundary must be much thinner than what flows within. Nations fail it. Trade, migration, science, film and the internet carry enormous traffic across every border. And the Belgian map shows that the strongest boundaries in the flow of information do not even follow the borders. They follow the languages.

So the theory's verdict is that nations are modules, not minds. They are real and they matter, as the visual cortex is real and matters. But the visual cortex does not see. The brain sees. In the same sense a nation does not think. It is a region of something that thinks.

That something has no self either. Its sensory intake, eight billion pairs of eyes, vastly exceeds what flows between its members. By the measures of this book, humanity is intelligent in the way a swarm is intelligent, and conscious in the way a swarm is conscious, which is to say, as far as the theory can tell, not at all. There is thinking going on at the scale of the species. Nobody is doing it.

## One species among millions

Humanity is not the only candidate for a mind above the individual. Chapter 9 argued that every species is one: organisms exchanging information through their genes, learning at about a bit per generation, and dividing when their codes drift apart. What sets ours apart is speed. On top of the genes we built language and then writing, each many orders of magnitude faster than the last. Humanity is a species-mind that runs at the speed of speech, multiplied by eight billion, and it is the only one we can hope to listen to.

## Listening to a larger mind

If humanity thinks, it must be saying something to itself. Chapter 10 warned that minds running at very different speeds cannot perceive one another, so we should not expect to overhear it directly. But the theory constrains what a language at any level has to be like, and that tells us where to listen.

### The grammar of a higher level

Look again at the two languages we know from the inside out: the brain's, spoken by neurons, and the swarm's, spoken by bees. They share features that follow from the funnel, from shared context and from the crossing time. The theory predicts the same features at every level. These are hypotheses.

1. **Its words are populations, not utterances.** In the brain, the signal is how many neurons in a group are firing. In the swarm, it is how many scouts are dancing for a site. No single neuron or bee says the word. Humanity's words should likewise be counts and flows: how many people are buying, moving, reading, citing.
2. **A word lasts about as long as the mind's crossing time.** Milliseconds in a brain, hours in a swarm. As a collective's crossing time shrinks, its words should get shorter.
3. **The vocabulary is tiny.** A message is a pointer and a magnitude: *this site, this good*.
4. **Signals fade unless renewed**, as a scout's dance shortens with every return.
5. **Rival messages suppress one another**, as the scouts' stop signals do.
6. **It is unreadable without the shared context**, and so looks like noise from outside.
7. **Those who speak it do not hear it.** Each agent knows only its own act.

### Words we have already recorded

Three of these have been measured in human society, by researchers who were not looking for a language.

**The price.** In 1945 the economist Friedrich Hayek asked how a society makes use of knowledge that no single person possesses. His example was tin (8). Suppose that somewhere a new use for tin appears, or a mine fails. It does not matter which, he pointed out, and that it does not matter is the important thing. The price of tin rises. All over the world people who know nothing of the cause use a little less tin and look for substitutes. The whole acts as one market, he argued, not because any of its members survey the whole field, but because their limited fields of vision overlap enough for the relevant information to reach everyone. A price is a single number, a magnitude attached to a pointer. It is set by a population and spoken by nobody. And those who respond to it do not know what it says. It is features 1, 3 and 7 exactly. A price is a word in the language of humanity.

**The fading.** Researchers tracked how attention to cultural products declines over time: citations to papers and patents, and online attention to songs, films and biographies. In every case the decline followed the same curve, a fast decay followed by a slow one (9). They interpreted the two parts as two kinds of memory: one carried by people talking, which fades quickly, and one carried by records, which fades slowly. That is the bees' fading dance at human scale. Its two channels are the two that Chapter 10 distinguished: live coupling, and memory.

**The shortening.** Another group measured how long topics hold collective attention, across Twitter, books, web searches, films and Wikipedia, over periods of years to decades. Bursts of attention are becoming steeper and shorter, and occurring more often. A model in which topics compete for a limited supply of attention reproduces the data (10). The theory predicts this from feature 2: humanity's crossing time has collapsed, so its words are getting shorter. And the competition for finite attention is feature 5, cross-inhibition, under another name.

### An electroencephalogram for humanity

These hints suggest an experiment, and it is one that could be started tomorrow with public data.

A neurologist listens to a brain by placing electrodes over its regions and recording each one's activity. This chapter has argued that humanity's regions are its language communities. So place an electrode on each: record, hour by hour, the activity of each language edition of a large public resource, such as the page views of Wikipedia in each of its languages. Then ask of this recording what Chapter 3 asked of the cortex.

- **Does activity spread in avalanches?** In brain tissue, cascades of activity have a characteristic range of sizes, the signature of a system balanced at a critical point (11). Do cascades of attention across languages show the same pattern?
- **Does humanity decide the way swarms and brains do?** When rival alternatives compete for attention, does support for each build up toward a threshold while suppressing the other?
- **Is it the same thought at a different speed?** Rescale time by humanity's crossing time. Do its curves fall on top of those from swarms and brains, as Chapter 10 predicts?
- **How integrated is it, and how differentiated?** Doctors probe a brain by sending in a magnetic pulse and measuring the complexity of the echo: widespread *and* varied means awake, local or uniform means not (12). Humanity receives such pulses regularly, in the form of sudden world events. Measure the echo across languages in the same way.

The last test would give a number to this chapter's warning. Tracked over the years, a rising score would mean a species becoming more integrated while staying diverse. A falling one, caused by every language community reacting to everything in the same way at the same moment, would be the signature of the civilisational seizure described below.

Two cautions are needed. An event that reaches every community at once from outside is like a flash of light on the retina: it is input, not internal conversation, and the analysis has to separate the two. And the readers of any one website are not humanity. But nothing in this programme requires new instruments. For the first time, a larger mind has left its recordings lying about in public.

## The bottleneck has moved

For nearly all of history, humanity's crossing time, the time for news to get from one side of the collective to the other, was months. A letter from Lisbon to Macau travelled by sail.

Today light crosses half the planet's circumference in under a tenth of a second. Relative to the speed of its members' thoughts, humanity's wiring is now faster than the wiring of a brain.

And yet humanity does not think millions of times faster than it did in 1700. The theory says why. The wires were never the only limit. Every message still has to pass through a human being, at ten bits per second, and through the thin junctions between language groups. The internet removed the latency. It did not remove the funnel.

This identifies precisely which technologies would change what humanity *is*, as opposed to merely speeding up its mail. There are two.

**Machine translation raises the context shared between language modules.** In the terms of Chapter 6, it raises κ across the thinnest junctions in the species. The theory predicts that this integrates humanity more than any cable did.

**Artificial agents can bypass the funnel.** A machine can take part in the collective conversation with an output not of ten bits per second but of millions. If such agents become a large part of the traffic, the ratio that makes humanity a federation, thick agents and thin wires, begins to fall for the first time in history.

## Small enough to be one

How far could that go? Here physics has something surprising to say.

Chapter 4 showed that a mind can act as one only if signals cross it faster than it thinks. That sets a maximum size for a unified mind: the distance light travels during one of its thoughts.

Take a thought at human speed, about three tenths of a second. Light covers 90,000 kilometres in that time. The Earth is 12,700 kilometres across.

The Earth is small enough to be a single mind thinking at the speed we think. The laws of physics do not forbid a planetary self. What has prevented one, so far, is only the narrowness of its agents' voices.

This is a claim about what is possible, not about what is coming or what would be good. And the theory attaches a warning to it. Chapter 3 described what happens to a brain when coupling rises too far: every neuron fires in step, and the result is not a greater mind but a seizure. Chapter 6 found the same failure in meaning: a collective that agrees about everything can no longer learn anything. A humanity bound tightly enough to act as one, and carelessly enough to think alike, would not be a superintelligence. It would be eight billion people having the same thought, which is one thought.

The design problem of the coming century, stated in this book's terms, is therefore exact. Raise the shared code. Protect the diversity of belief. Integration without independence is the most dangerous state a collective mind can reach, and it is the one that our tools make easiest.

## The Moon is too far

Now step off the planet. The same ceiling that permits a mind the size of the Earth forbids almost everything larger.

| Speed of thought | Largest mind that can think as one | For comparison |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A millisecond (a fast machine) | about 300 km | a cluster of cities |
| Three tenths of a second (a person) | about 90,000 km | seven Earths side by side |
| A day | about 170 times the Earth–Sun distance | beyond all the planets |
| A year | one light-year | a quarter of the way to the nearest star |
| 100,000 years | 100,000 light-years | the Milky Way |

The Moon is 384,000 kilometres away, 1.3 light-seconds. A mind thinking at human speed cannot include it. An outpost there would be like a hand with a one-second delay: usable, but no longer quite part of you.

Mars is far worse. A radio signal takes between about 3 and 22 minutes to get there, depending on where the two planets are in their orbits (13). A question and its answer can take three quarters of an hour. A civilisation on two planets is, by the arithmetic of this book, two minds that write to each other. For any decision faster than an hour, each planet is on its own.

So the first step into space is also a step back down the ladder of integration. Expansion and unity pull in opposite directions, and the speed of light sets the exchange rate between them.

## What a galactic mind would be like

Could humanity, or anything, become an intelligence on the scale of the galaxy? The theory's answer is yes, and it can describe the result in some detail, because the constraints leave so little freedom. What follows is speculation, but it is disciplined speculation: each feature is forced by something earlier in the book.

**It would be a federation.** Each star system would be a nearly complete mind in itself, taking in vastly more than it could ever send. The galaxy would be to its star systems as the swarm is to its bees.

**It would be slow beyond imagining.** Chapter 10 estimated a few million years per decision, and a few thousand decisions in the entire age of the galaxy. Its present moment would be a hundred thousand years wide.

**It could act only on slow things.** A mind can keep pace only with what changes more slowly than it does. By the argument of Chapter 10, a galactic mind could still know about a war or a species, from the records its fast members keep. But it could do nothing about them in time. The only things it could steer would be the lives of stars and the chemistry of spiral arms.

**It would speak almost never, and say almost nothing new.** With crossing times of millennia, bandwidth is nearly worthless and shared context is everything. By the argument of Chapter 6, its members would hold deep models of one another and communicate by exception: a message only when something departs from prediction. Silence would be its normal state, and silence would mean *all is as expected*.

**Its messages would look like noise.** A perfectly compressed signal has no pattern that an outsider can find. A message that relies on a million years of shared context is, to anyone without that context, indistinguishable from static.

**It would prefer the cold.** The energy cost of handling a bit is proportional to temperature. Computation in deep space, at under three degrees above absolute zero, is about a hundred times cheaper per bit than at room temperature. Some researchers have gone further and proposed that advanced civilisations would wait for the universe itself to cool, to gain a far larger factor (14). That argument is disputed on thermodynamic grounds (15), but the direction of the pressure is not: thinking is cheaper far from stars.

Put these together and you get a portrait: quiet, cold, slow, sparse, its rare signals disguised as noise. It is worth noticing that this is also a fair description of what the sky looks like. The theory does not claim that the galaxy is inhabited by such a mind. It claims something narrower and testable in principle: that if large, old intelligences exist, the physics of this book makes them very hard to see for creatures who think in seconds. The famous silence of the sky is what the theory would expect in either case, and so, by itself, it is evidence of nothing.

## Could we become one?

Humanity could seed such a thing. It could not become it and remain itself.

The reason is the one that split the warblers in Chapter 9. A collective stays one only while its traffic outruns its drift. Human languages drift beyond mutual understanding in something like a thousand years (a rough figure). Two settlements whose messages take longer than that to cross between them cannot keep a common tongue, however hard they try. That puts the radius of a single human culture at a few hundred light-years at the very most, a small neighbourhood in a galaxy a hundred thousand light-years wide. Beyond it, colonies would become foreign to each other, and over longer spans, separated by more than gene flow can bridge, they would become different species. The galaxy would not be settled by humanity. It would be settled by humanity's descendants, who would be to one another what the two warblers of Siberia are: cousins who no longer know each other's song. Each expansion beyond the reach of light within a thought creates a new, separate mind that must be reconnected by context instead of by speed. A human galactic civilisation would be a family of minds that drift apart and are held together only by what they shared before they parted, and by rare, dense letters.

There are, in the end, two kinds of greatness on offer, and physics does not let any mind have both. A mind can be unified, fast and present, and then it can be no bigger than a planet. Or it can be vast, and then it must be plural, slow and mostly silent. We are living, by coincidence or not, at the moment when a species first has to decide which of the two it is building.

## In short

- Humanity has the ingredients of a collective mind: eight billion agents, shared codes, external memory. It is of the federated type, intelligent without being a self. Science is its clearest thought process.
- Its modules follow shared language more than political borders, as phone-call networks and translation networks show. By the theory's test, nations are modules, not minds.
- Any higher-level language should consist of population-level signals with a tiny vocabulary that fade, compete and go unheard by those who produce them. Prices, the decay of collective attention and its shortening bursts fit. Recording each language community's activity would allow humanity to be tested like a brain.
- Humanity's wiring is now fast. Its bottleneck is the human output of about ten bits per second and the thin junctions between languages. Machine translation and artificial agents act on exactly those two limits.
- The speed of light caps a unified mind at the distance light travels in one of its thoughts. At human speed that is 90,000 km: enough for the Earth, not for the Moon.
- Tighter integration risks the collective version of a seizure: uniform belief. The goal is shared code with diverse belief.
- A galactic intelligence is physically possible only as a slow, cold, nearly silent federation with a budget of a few thousand decisions in the age of the galaxy.

## How we could be wrong

The claim that languages are humanity's modules predicts that boundaries in communication networks follow language more closely than they follow borders. Wider data could show the opposite. The claim that nations are not selves depends on a measure, the ratio of cross-border to internal information flow counted in meaning, that has not been computed. The claim that the human funnel, not latency, limits humanity's speed of thought predicts that faster networks alone will not shorten the time global questions take to settle, while better translation and machine participation will. The light-speed ceiling would fail only if relativity did. Everything said about galactic minds is extrapolation from laws tested at far smaller scales, and no observation currently distinguishes a galaxy with such a mind from one without.

The account of humanity's language is also testable. If activity across language communities shows no avalanche pattern, if collective choices show no build-up and mutual suppression, or if the curves refuse to collapse onto those of swarms and brains when time is rescaled, then humanity does not think in the way this chapter claims, however much it talks.
