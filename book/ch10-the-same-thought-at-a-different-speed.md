# Chapter 10. The same thought at a different speed

## The aeroplane on a stick

Before an airliner is built, a model of it about a metre long is mounted on a rod in a wind tunnel. Engineers measure the forces on the model and use them to predict how three hundred tonnes of aircraft will behave in the sky.

This should not work. Air does not know it is flowing past a model. It has its own stickiness and its own density, and those do not shrink with the aeroplane. A small, slow model sits in a completely different flow from a large, fast aircraft.

It works because of a discovery from the nineteenth century. What the flow does depends on a pure number with no units, the Reynolds number, which combines the size, the speed and the properties of the fluid. If the model and the aircraft have the same Reynolds number, their flows are the same flow: every swirl around the model has its exact counterpart around the aircraft, larger and slower. Physicists say the two are *dynamically similar*. Size and speed are not what a flow is. The pure number is.

This book has been collecting pure numbers for minds. This chapter asks the question that the wind tunnel makes unavoidable. If two minds of very different sizes have the same numbers, are they thinking the same thought?

## A clock that belongs to the mind

To compare a mind the size of a walnut with one the size of a tree, we need to stop using our own clock.

Every collective has a natural unit of time: how long a signal takes to cross it, from one side to the other. For a human brain this crossing time is a few hundredths of a second. For a swarm, where a "signal" is a bee flying to a site and coming back to dance, it is tens of minutes. Nothing coordinated can happen in a collective faster than its crossing time, so this is the tick of its clock.

Define a mind's **cognitive time** as ordinary time divided by its crossing time. Cognitive time counts ticks. One second is perhaps thirty ticks for your brain and a small fraction of one tick for a swarm.

The proposal, which the theory calls the similarity principle, is this:

> Two minds are thinking the same thought when, measured in cognitive time, their states of information pass through the same sequence, and that happens when their pure numbers match.

The thought is the pattern. The wall-clock duration is an accident of the hardware.

## The monkey and the swarm

There is already one place to test this. Chapter 1 described two decisions that use the same algorithm: a monkey deciding which way a cloud of dots is drifting, and a swarm deciding where to live. In both, rival populations build up evidence, suppress each other, and stop when one crosses a threshold.

Count the ticks.

- The monkey takes roughly half a second. Signals hop between neurons in something like five to ten milliseconds, so the decision takes on the order of **50 to 100 steps**.
- The swarm in Seeley's 1997 observations took about 16 hours of dancing. A scout's round trip, out to the site, in to inspect it, back to dance, takes tens of minutes, so the decision took on the order of **20 to 50 steps**.

These are rough estimates and should be treated as such. But look at what they say. The two decisions differ in duration by a factor of about a hundred thousand. Measured in each system's own ticks, they are within a small factor of each other. Both minds decide in tens of steps.

A melody is the same melody whether it is played in three minutes by a piccolo or in three hours by foghorns. If the similarity principle holds, the monkey and the swarm are playing the same piece.

## Why the copy is rarely perfect

Engineers know that similarity has limits, and the limits apply to minds too.

**The world does not slow down for you.** A thought has to be about something, and the something has its own pace. The swarm's slow deliberation works because a hollow tree stays put for decades. A swarm could not use the same thought, at its speed, to catch a ball. A mind can only keep pace, in real time, with parts of the world that change more slowly than it ticks. Keeping pace is not the same as thinking about, and the difference matters enough to deserve its own section below.

**You cannot match every number at once.** Ship designers face a famous version of this. A model hull in a towing tank should match two pure numbers, one for waves and one for friction, and with water as the fluid it is impossible to match both in a small model. They match the one that matters most and correct for the other. Minds face the same bind, because some quantities refuse to scale: the speed of light, the temperature of the surroundings, the energy cost of a bit. A galaxy-sized mind cannot be a brain enlarged. Some of its numbers must differ, and so some of its thoughts must differ in kind.

**The thought has to fit.** The same thought needs the same information. A collective with a small memory or narrow channels can hold only a compressed version, the way a melody survives being whistled but a symphony does not.

So the honest statement is that exact copies are rare and family resemblances are common. The theory's prediction is specific, though: where two systems differ, the mismatched number says in which direction their thoughts will differ.

## How a slow mind thinks about fast things

There is an obvious objection to the first of those limits. We think about things far faster than ourselves all the time. A physicist reasons about electrons, whose states change in a few tenths of an attosecond, and an attosecond is to one second roughly what one second is to the age of the universe. If a mind were limited to subjects slower than its own tick, physics would be impossible.

The answer is that there are two different relations a mind can have with a part of the world, and the speed limit applies to only one of them.

**Coupling** is following events as they happen and acting on them one by one. This is what the batter does with the ball, and it is strictly limited by the crossing time. No human will ever watch an electron move.

**Modelling** is holding a description of something. A description does not have to change as fast as the thing it describes. Usually it does not change at all. That is how slow minds reach fast things, and they do it in four ways.

1. **Turn time into space.** For years people argued about whether a galloping horse ever has all four hooves off the ground. The gallop is too fast for the eye. In June 1878 Eadweard Muybridge set a row of cameras along a racetrack in Palo Alto, each triggered as the horse passed, with shutters as fast as a thousandth of a second. The photographs showed the horse airborne. Nobody's perception got faster. The event was converted into a row of pictures that sit still on a table and can be studied for an hour. Every oscilloscope trace, every graph with time along one axis, is the same trick: a record is a fast thing made slow. The attosecond pulses that won the 2023 Nobel Prize in physics are Muybridge's cameras, a million billion times quicker.
2. **Think about what does not change.** The path of one air molecule is hopelessly fast. The law that governs it is not fast at all; it is the same now as it was a billion years ago. A law of nature is the slowest object there is. When a physicist thinks about electrons she is thinking about the rule they follow, and the rule holds still.
3. **Average.** Temperature is a summary of trillions of collisions per second. The summary changes slowly even though what it summarises does not. Chapter 3 said the air in your kitchen is not a mind, but you can still think about its temperature.
4. **Delegate downward.** When your hand pulls back from the stove, a faster and smaller part of you has dealt with a problem the whole could not reach in time. We now build such parts on purpose. A camera, a particle detector and a computer are fast agents that couple to the fast world on our behalf and report back at ten bits per second.

Notice that the first three all run through memory. A photograph, an equation and a statistic are stored things. So the speed limit on thought is really two limits with different remedies. What a mind can *react to* is set by its crossing time. What a mind can *think about* is set by its memory and its instruments. A mind with a good enough memory can think about anything, fast or slow, provided something else catches it first.

The same trick works in the other direction, and this is easy to miss. We also think about things far *slower* than ourselves: the drift of continents, the evolution of species. No person lives long enough to watch them. We reach them through records too, laid down by rock strata and by generations of other people. Writing is to slow things what Muybridge's cameras are to fast ones.

That has a consequence for the hierarchy of minds, and it is a claim of the theory. A small fast mind and a large slow mind are not sealed off from each other's subjects. The fast one can think about slow things by reading the records the slow one keeps. The slow one can think about fast things by reading the summaries the fast ones send up. That is exactly the traffic that flows between the levels of a collective: summaries going up, records coming down. What neither can do is *act* in the other's time. You can understand an ice age. You cannot do anything at the speed of one, and it is humanity, not you, that will have to respond to the next.

## How many thoughts does a mind get?

If a decision costs a mind some tens of its own ticks, then a mind's lifetime, divided by its decision time, is the number of decisions it will ever make. Call it the mind's thought budget. The figures below are order-of-magnitude estimates, using about fifty ticks per decision.

| Mind | Time per decision | Lifetime | Thought budget |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A person | about half a second | about 80 years | about 5 billion |
| A bee swarm choosing a home | about a day | a few days as a homeless swarm | one, perhaps a few |
| Humanity, on a global question | decades | about 5,000 years since writing | a few hundred |
| A mind spanning the galaxy | about 5 million years | 13 billion years, the age of the galaxy | about 2,600 |

Read the last row again. Light takes roughly 100,000 years to cross the Milky Way. Fifty crossings is five million years. If a single mind had spanned our galaxy since the galaxy formed, it would by now have completed fewer deliberate decisions than you make before lunch.

A person is a small, fast, short-lived mind with an enormous budget of thoughts. A galaxy would be a vast, slow, ancient mind with a tiny one. Neither is the lesser. They are spending the same currency on different things: the person on billions of thoughts about a small, quick world, the galaxy on a few thousand thoughts about everything.

The row for humanity deserves a moment too. If global questions really take decades to settle, then in all of recorded history our species, thinking as a whole, has made a few hundred decisions. The abolition of slavery might be one. The acceptance that the Earth goes round the Sun might be another. This is a speculative way to count, but it changes how the pace of history feels. Humanity is not slow to act. It is a large mind acting at the speed its size allows.

## The width of now

Cognitive time gives a strange twist to an ordinary word. For any mind, "now" cannot be thinner than its crossing time, because within that window its parts have not yet heard from each other.

Your now is a few tenths of a second wide. Experiments on perception find just such a window, inside which the brain binds events together as simultaneous. A swarm's now is the better part of an hour. Humanity's now is debatable, but it is certainly not an instant. And for a mind spanning the galaxy, now would be a hundred thousand years wide. The whole of human history, from the first cave paintings to this sentence, would fit inside one of its present moments with room to spare.

## Minds that cannot see each other

This leads to the chapter's most unsettling consequence, and it is speculation.

A neuron takes part in your thoughts and has no access to them. Your thought is a pattern across billions of neurons over half a second. From where the neuron sits, there are only pulses arriving and pulses leaving.

The same blindness should hold between any two levels whose clocks differ enough. A mind that ticks a hundred thousand times slower than we do would be invisible to us as a mind. We would see only its parts, doing what parts do. And we would be invisible to it: too small, too fast, a shimmer. It could no more notice an individual human life than you can notice one neuron firing once.

So the question "are we part of a larger thought?" is not mystical in this theory. It is a question with a method. The larger thought, if it exists, would show up as a pattern in the slow, coarse variables of the collective: the things that take decades to change and that no individual controls. We would not perceive it. We could, in principle, measure it.

## A test

The similarity principle makes a prediction of a kind physicists like, because it is easy to fail.

Take many systems that make the same type of decision: brains, swarms, ant colonies, committees, juries. For each, measure how accuracy improves as the system is given more time. Plotted against ordinary time, the curves will be scattered across many orders of magnitude. Now replot each one against its own cognitive time. If the principle is right, the curves for systems with similar pure numbers will fall on top of one another, forming a single curve. If they stay scattered, the principle is wrong.

This technique is called data collapse. It is how physicists established that very different materials behave identically near a critical point. Nobody has yet tried it on minds.

## In short

- Scale models work because a flow is defined by pure numbers, not by size or speed. The same may be true of thoughts.
- Every mind has its own clock, set by how long a signal takes to cross it. Cognitive time counts those ticks.
- The similarity principle: two minds with matching pure numbers think the same thought, each at its own speed.
- A monkey's brain and a bee swarm use the same decision algorithm and take a similar number of their own steps, tens, although their durations differ about a hundred-thousand-fold. These counts are rough estimates.
- Similarity is usually partial, because the environment has its own pace and some physical constants do not scale.
- The speed limit applies to reacting, not to thinking about. Minds reach faster and slower things through records, unchanging laws, averages and delegated agents. All of these depend on memory.
- Consequences: every mind has a finite budget of thoughts, about five billion for a person and a few thousand for a galaxy; every mind's "now" has a width; minds at very different speeds would be invisible to each other.

## How we could be wrong

The principle fails if the data-collapse test fails: if decisions of the same type take wildly different numbers of crossing times in different systems, with no pattern explained by their other pure numbers. The step counts quoted for the monkey and the swarm are estimates; careful measurement could move them apart. The thought budgets depend on the assumption of roughly fifty ticks per decision and inherit its uncertainty. The idea that subjective time follows cognitive time is not yet testable at all.
